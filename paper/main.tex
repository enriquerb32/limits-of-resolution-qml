\begin{abstract}
Quantum machine learning (QML) benchmarks on small tabular datasets are usually read as rankings of architectures, often from differences of a few hundredths of AUC. We ask how large a difference such a benchmark can resolve and how much the verdict depends on the inferential procedure. On a Western Mediterranean precipitation panel (four stations, 1938--2026; 528 test station-months), one AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, 4--7 times the seed standard deviation of Random Forest, XGBoost and an LSTM, so more seeds cannot remove the uncertainty of a finite test set. The minimum detectable difference of 15-seed means is 0.031--0.044 AUC and falls only as $N^{-1/2}$ with test size. Near this limit the verdict depends on the procedure: for Random Forest--LSTM ($+0.015$) the year-block bootstrap interval excludes zero while DeLong does not ($p=0.19$), and known-truth simulations show that each procedure can be miscalibrated under plausible dependence between test years. In a case study with matched fitting rows and 15 seeds per model, four hybrid quantum models scored 0.020--0.026 AUC below Random Forest and were not distinguished from a parameter-matched, circuit-free classical control; deciding whether the circuit adds anything would need five to seven times more test data. No model, classical or quantum, improved on a month$\times$station climatology, and within stations the climatology discriminated best. We propose a reporting protocol for small QML benchmarks: a stated estimand, a minimum detectable difference computed before ranking models, separate seed and sampling error, a base-rate reference and a classical control at the same interface.

\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Quantum Machine Learning, Benchmarking, Statistical Power, Minimum Detectable Effect, Seed Variance, Precipitation Forecasting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Claims of quantum utility on classical tabular data frequently rest on a single training run per model, capacity-asymmetric classical baselines, and uncertainty estimates that cover only one source of error \citep{bowles2024}. The machine-learning literature has long pointed out that a single run confounds initialization luck with architectural merit \citep{henderson2018,bouthillier2021}, and that tests computed on one fixed test set ignore the variance induced by the training procedure \citep{dietterich1998,demsar2006}. Variational circuits aggravate the issue: in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era \citep{preskill2018}, parameterized quantum circuits are sensitive to initialization and, at larger qubit counts, prone to barren plateaus \citep{mcclean2018}, and they are usually combined with a classical backbone that compresses the input into a few qubits \citep{huang2021,schuld2022}.

Throughout, ``noise'' means statistical variability (test-set sampling error and optimization-seed variance), not quantum hardware noise. This paper asks a design question that comes before any ranking: how large a difference in AUC can a benchmark with a few hundred test cases resolve, and how much does the verdict on a small difference depend on the inferential procedure? The answer is largely a property of the evaluation design rather than of the application. The standard error of one AUC is set by the size and prevalence of the test set (about 0.024 for the 528 test cases studied here); it is several times the variation between training seeds of the classical models, and it falls only as $N^{-1/2}$, so repeating training with more seeds does not remove it. For differences of a few hundredths, the paired standard error, the dependence between test cases and the choice of procedure decide the verdict. Variational hybrids add a second complication: their seed variance is large enough that seed and sampling error must both be reported.

We work through these questions on a deliberately modest but realistic case: a multi-decadal, multi-station Western Mediterranean precipitation panel, an area where heavy precipitation is linked to cut-off lows aloft (DANAs; \citealp{romero1999,nieto2005}) and where tree ensembles are strong baselines on engineered tabular features \citep{grinsztajn2022}. The task is a 1-month-ahead probabilistic forecast of precipitation exceeding a pooled regional 60th percentile ($P_{60}=\qty{28.1}{\milli\meter}$) from antecedent 12-month features. The panel has the features that make small benchmarks hard to read: a few hundred correlated test cases, strong base-rate structure across sites and seasons, and labels affected by missing data. It is a testbed for evaluation methodology, not an operational early-warning system.

Applied climate- and weather-QML studies have addressed separate parts of the problem: opportunities for climate modeling \citep{schwabe2025op}, parameter-count and initialization-matched comparisons for cloud cover \citep{schwabe2025,pastori2026}, wind-speed forecasting \citep{dasilva2026}, quantum kernels for climate emulation \citep{bazgir2024}, synthetic time-series benchmarks \citep{fellner2026}, variational data assimilation \citep{kumar2026}, and parameter-matched neural comparisons \citep{marchisio2026}. In the time-series and weather QML studies cited above, quantum models are reported as better than, or as having the potential to outperform, classical baselines on the basis of point estimates: \citet{marchisio2026} report a 15.4\,\% lower MSE than a parameter-matched classical leaky-integrate-and-fire neuron on a 2{,}000-sample test set, with one value per model and no interval or test, and \citet{dasilva2026} use 10 runs per configuration and show their spread in violin plots, but compare the models through single error values with no interval or test, although their test sets span only 14 and 5 days. We do not claim that these effects are spurious; the point is that the evidence reported cannot separate them from sampling and seed noise. This paper complements these studies by asking how large a difference an evaluation design can resolve.

\textbf{What the paper offers to other benchmarks.} The statistical tools are standard (DeLong, cluster bootstrap, minimum detectable difference); the contribution is to combine them around two explicit estimands and to quantify what they imply for benchmark design. Three results transfer beyond this panel. First, a resolution rule, checked empirically on the panel: the minimum detectable AUC difference (MDE) of two models compared on the same $N$ test cases falls as $N^{-1/2}$, from about 0.03 for 15-seed means at $N\approx500$ to about 0.016 at $N\approx2{,}000$ (\Cref{sec:design}). Second, near this limit verdicts depend on the procedure, and known-truth simulations show under which dependence structures each procedure is miscalibrated (\Cref{sec:simulation}). Third, a reporting protocol whose items need only the test predictions of the models (\Cref{tab:protocol}).

\textbf{What the paper does not claim.} It does not rank the architectures. In particular, it does not show that Random Forest, or tree ensembles in general, beat quantum or neural models on tabular data: the Random Forest--hybrid gap is resolved by some procedures and not by others, Random Forest only matches a month$\times$station climatology, and within a station the climatology discriminates best (\Cref{sec:not_show}). For the same reason the panel is a poor testbed for choosing an architecture, since on it no learned model adds resolvable skill; it is used here for the statistical question, for which small differences between models are the case of interest (\Cref{sec:testbed}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A noise budget with two explicit estimands}, the difference of single-run AUCs and of seed-averaged AUCs, with paired DeLong, year-block bootstrap and two-way (years and seeds) bootstrap inference, for three classical models and four hybrid quantum models on 528 test station-months (\Cref{sec:framework,sec:budget}).
    \item \textbf{Known-truth simulations} of two correlated scorers with shared and model-specific year effects, which check the type-I error, coverage and power of the procedures at this sample size (\Cref{sec:simulation}).
    \item \textbf{A resolution analysis for benchmark design}, combining a closed-form approximation with an empirical curve of the minimum detectable difference against test size (\Cref{sec:design}).
    \item \textbf{A QML case study and a reporting protocol}: four hybrid quantum models, a circuit-free classical control at the same four-dimensional interface, a standalone LSTM and Random Forest, fitted on the same rows with 15 seeds each, compared against climatological references on discrimination, proper scoring and within-station discrimination, with an audit of label validity (\Cref{sec:labels,sec:reference,sec:applying,sec:protocol}).
\end{enumerate}
Further material (full label audit, positive control, single-seed analyses, historical runs with other architectures, ablations, operational metrics, circuit costs) is in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Data, Task and Models}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Dataset, Target and Split}
\label{sec:dataset_merge}

The study domain is the Spanish Mediterranean coast (Catalonia, the Valencian Community and the Region of Murcia), a transition zone between Mediterranean (\textit{Csa}) and semi-arid (\textit{BSk}/\textit{BSh}) K\"{o}ppen regimes subject to severe convective episodes \citep{romero1999,nieto2005}. Daily records of four AEMET stations, Alacant/Alicante, Barcelona Aeropuerto, Val\`{e}ncia and San Javier Aeropuerto (\Cref{tab:station_coverage}), were obtained from \textit{datosclima.es} under the AEMET Open Data framework (downloaded on 1 June 2026; station metadata from the AEMET climatological normals pages, accessed 28 October 2026). They are aggregated to monthly values per station: precipitation sum, wettest day, days above the daily 90th percentile, mean station-level pressure (not reduced to sea level) and its variability, temperature and diurnal range, wind speed and maximum gust, sunshine duration, and reference evapotranspiration from FAO-56 Penman--Monteith with humidity estimated from the minimum temperature, radiation from sunshine duration and a constant wind speed $u_2=\qty{2}{\meter\per\second}$ \citep{allen1998}. Months with fewer than 10 valid daily records are excluded; monthly totals are sums over the available days, so incomplete months remain in the panel (\Cref{sec:labels}). The panel has 3{,}363 station-months; after a 12-month antecedent window per station it yields 3{,}315 prediction windows. Inhomogeneities from relocations or instrument changes remain an accepted limitation \citep{alexandersson1986,peterson1998}.

Each window uses months $i,\dots,i+11$ (35 features: 12 precipitation lags, 17 engineered physical features, 2 cyclic month terms and 4 station indicators) to predict whether precipitation in month $i+12$ exceeds the pooled regional 60th percentile, $P_{60}=\qty{28.1}{\milli\meter}$, computed on the training partition. The split is chronological: training 1938--1996 (2{,}273 windows), validation 1997--2007 (514) and test 2008--2026 (528; 41.1\,\% positive). Because records are joined across five daily variable files, San Javier and Valencia leave the panel when their pressure series end (2010 and 2012), and 83.7\,\% of the test windows come from Alicante and Barcelona. Test base rates differ between stations (29--56\,\%), so part of the pooled AUC reflects telling stations apart (\Cref{sec:reference}). All scaling and anomaly statistics use pre-1997 data only; computing the anomalies this way rather than from the full record moves the AUC of each model by 0.003 or less (\Cref{S-app:leakage_correction}). The test period falls in a time of accelerated warming, so the evaluation takes place under a covariate shift whose effect cannot be isolated with one chronological split.

\subsection{Models and Fitting Sets}
\label{sec:validation_protocol}
\label{sec:architectures}

\textbf{Models in the main analyses.} Two references, a month$\times$station climatology fitted on the training partition and persistence (lag-1 precipitation), are compared with three classical models, Random Forest (300 trees, depth 8; \citealp{breiman2001}), XGBoost (500 trees, depth 4) and a standalone bidirectional LSTM (10{,}103 parameters), and with four hybrid quantum--classical models simulated without noise in PennyLane. \texttt{LSTM+QNN} compresses a 51-dimensional vector (the 16-dimensional LSTM state concatenated with the 35 features) to 4 rotation angles that feed a 4-qubit circuit, which computes a truncated Fourier series of these angles \citep{schuld2021fourier}; it has 2{,}613 parameters, 24 of them quantum. \texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN} is the same model trained with an additional hand-set penalty loosely motivated by the Clausius--Clapeyron relation. Its constants are not derived from physics and are not dimensionally consistent, and in the implementation the penalty acts as a fixed calendar prior against confident rain forecasts in winter and spring (\Cref{S-app:heuristic_regularization}). We therefore treat \texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN} as a regularization variant of \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, not as a physics-informed model, and draw no conclusion about physics-informed learning from it. \texttt{LbL-QNN} a 3-layer circuit trained layer by layer \citep{skolik2021}, and \texttt{LSTM+QTL} a quantum head on a frozen pre-trained LSTM \citep{mari2020}. The classical control has the architecture of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} with the circuit replaced by a linear $4\to4$ map (20 parameters), so it isolates the contribution of the circuit at the same interface. The models differ in capacity: the standalone LSTM has 10{,}103 trainable parameters, the hybrids 2{,}613--2{,}625 (\texttt{LSTM+QTL} trains 617 on top of its frozen LSTM) and the control 2{,}609. Comparisons of a hybrid with the standalone LSTM or with Random Forest are therefore comparisons of whole pipelines and cannot be attributed to the circuit; the comparison that isolates the circuit is the one with the control, whose parameter count matches that of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} to within four parameters. Architectures and hyperparameters are given in \Cref{S-app:architectures,S-app:hyperparams}, and parameter counts in \Cref{S-tab:parameter_accounting}. Logistic regression, gradient boosting, a quantum-kernel SVM (whose product-state kernel factorizes classically) and further bottleneck and encoding variants appear only in the historical runs of the Supplement (\Cref{S-app:historical}).

\textbf{Fitting sets and seeds.} All models in the main analyses use the same 2{,}273 training rows, the neural and hybrid models use the 514 validation rows for early stopping, and all are scored on the same 528 test rows, with 15 seeds each; the seed index is nominal across frameworks. The Random Forest and LSTM fits of the classical and quantum experiments are identical (their test probabilities agree to within $10^{-11}$). The primary metric is the ROC-AUC, complemented by the Brier score \citep{brier1950}. Historical runs in the canonical pipeline, which standardized anomalies with the full record and fitted the tree ensembles on training plus validation (2{,}787 rows), are reported only descriptively; the extra rows change the Random Forest AUC by 0.003--0.007 (\Cref{S-app:leakage_correction}).

\subsection{Estimands and Inference}
\label{sec:estimand}

For models $A$ and $B$ with test probabilities $p_{A,s}$, $p_{B,s}$ from seeds $s=1,\dots,S$ ($S=15$), the main test statistic of this paper is the difference between seed-averaged test AUCs,
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta=\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{A,s})-\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{B,s}),
\end{equation}
and, for single-run comparisons, the difference of two individual AUCs. Sampling inference targets the corresponding performance difference for the evaluation population, conditional on the observed training data and on the fitted seed-specific models. The DeLong variance additionally conditions on the observed test years and treats station-months as exchangeable; the year-block bootstrap and the simulations of \Cref{sec:simulation} instead target the difference for new years, so a DeLong miscalibration in the simulations partly reflects this change of target. A bootstrap that also resamples seeds targets the seed-averaged difference over the distribution of optimization seeds, again conditional on the training data. The mean of AUCs is linear in the DeLong structural components, so the paired sampling variance of $\hat\Delta$ is obtained by averaging the components over seeds; this treats the seeds as fixed.

\subsection{Sampling Uncertainty}

Test-set sampling error is estimated in three ways. (i) The paired DeLong estimator for correlated ROC curves \citep{delong1988,hanley1982} treats station-months as independent and may therefore be optimistic for monthly data. (ii) A calendar-year block bootstrap resamples the 19 test years (2008--2026) with all stations of a year together ($B=2{,}000$; \citealp{kunsch1989,obuchowski1997}). Bootstrap confidence intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicated statistic, and bootstrap standard errors are the sample standard deviations of the replicates; resamples that contain only one class are discarded. Where a bootstrap $p$-value is reported, it is $2\min\{\Pr(\hat\Delta^{*}\le 0),\Pr(\hat\Delta^{*}\ge 0)\}$ over the replicates $\hat\Delta^{*}$, bounded below by $1/B$. (iii) A two-way bootstrap resamples years and, within each model, seeds. With only 19 clusters the block bootstrap is itself imprecise: cluster-based variance estimates are biased downward when clusters are few \citep{cameron2008}, the simulations of \Cref{sec:simulation} show a liberal block bootstrap (type-I error 0.074--0.084 at nominal 0.05), and for some pairs it returns a smaller standard error than DeLong. A rejection by the cluster procedures alone is therefore treated as provisional. All procedures are reported, and none is assumed a priori to be the correct one for the observational panel. As a check on the block standard error, a delete-one-year jackknife recomputes $\hat\Delta$ with each of the $K=19$ test years removed, $\text{SE}_{\text{jk}}=\{(K-1)/K\sum_k(\hat\Delta_{(k)}-\bar\Delta_{(\cdot)})^2\}^{1/2}$, with intervals $\hat\Delta\pm t_{0.975,18}\,\text{SE}_{\text{jk}}$ (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Seed Variability and the Seed Share}

For a model with seed standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{seed}}$ and mean sampling standard error $\text{SE}$ of one AUC we report $\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2/(\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2+\text{SE}^2)$. This is a descriptive ratio of two conditional variances (seeds, on a fixed test set; test sampling, for a fixed trained model), not a decomposition of the total uncertainty of a training procedure; it compares the scale of the two sources. For a difference between models $A$ and $B$, whose seeds are drawn independently, the seed variance is $\sigma_{A}^2+\sigma_{B}^2$ for one seed per model and $(\sigma_{A}^2+\sigma_{B}^2)/S$ for the difference of $S$-seed means, and SE is the corresponding paired sampling standard error (for one seed per model, averaged over the $S\times S$ seed pairings).

\subsection{Minimum Detectable Difference}

For a paired difference with standard error $\text{SE}_\Delta$, the minimum detectable difference at two-sided $\alpha=0.05$ and power 0.80 is
\begin{equation}
\text{MDE} = (z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\text{SE}_\Delta \approx 2.80\,\text{SE}_\Delta .
\end{equation}
Under a normal approximation with a locally constant standard error, the MDE describes the sensitivity of the design to a true difference: true differences below it are expected to be declared significant in fewer than 80\,\% of repeated experiments. It is not a threshold that an observed difference must exceed, and an observed gap between $1.96\,\text{SE}_\Delta$ and $2.80\,\text{SE}_\Delta$ can be nominally significant. We therefore report observed gaps with their intervals and use the MDE only to state which true differences the design would detect reliably.

\subsection{Scope of Claims and Multiplicity}
\label{sec:multiplicity}

The primary analyses are the noise budget, the paired comparisons, the simulations and the comparison with the climatology; none was pre-registered. Holm correction is applied to the DeLong $p$-values within two families of three seed-mean comparisons (the classical models, and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} against Random Forest, the control and the LSTM), and table captions quote the first-step threshold 0.017 for reference. Analyses added after the main analysis are secondary: the comparisons of the other three hybrids (\Cref{tab:hyb_paired}), the Brier scores (\Cref{tab:brier}), the within-station AUC (\Cref{tab:within}), the resolution curve (\Cref{tab:resolution}) and the block-length sensitivity. Bootstrap intervals are pointwise, and every nominal $p$-value outside the two Holm families is hypothesis-generating. Seed-level tests in the Supplement (Wilcoxon, Friedman) compare seeds on one fixed test set and describe optimization variance, not generalization.

\section{Results}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The Noise Budget}
\label{sec:budget}

We first measure the two sources of error. On the scale of one AUC, conditional test-sampling variability exceeds across-seed variability for all three classical models (\Cref{tab:budget}): one AUC has a standard error of 0.023--0.027 under either estimator, whereas the seed standard deviation is 0.004--0.006, so the seed accounts for 2--7\,\% of the combined seed-plus-sampling variance (a descriptive comparison of scales, not an estimate of the share of total uncertainty that more seeds could remove). The hybrid quantum models have larger seed standard deviations. The 15-seed \texttt{LSTM+QNN} of \Cref{sec:applying} has a seed standard deviation of 0.0098 and a seed share of 15\,\% (12\,\% with the block standard error; last column of \Cref{tab:budget}); for \texttt{LbL-QNN}, \texttt{LSTM+QTL} and \texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN}, fitted in the same experiment, the seed standard deviations are 0.0129, 0.0147 and 0.0170 and the seed shares 23, 27 and 34\,\% (block 18, 22 and 30\,\%). On the scale of one AUC the sampling term is the larger one for all the models analyzed, but for the hybrids the seed term is no longer small.

\Cref{tab:paired_ci} and \Cref{fig:paired_ci} compare the 15-seed means ($\Delta = +0.015$ for RF $-$ LSTM; the Random Forest and LSTM fits are the same as in the core experiment of \Cref{sec:applying}). The standard error of a paired difference is about half that of one AUC, and averaging the seeds lowers it further, but the DeLong MDE remains 0.031--0.044 (block estimates are 0.034, 0.017 and 0.043). For RF $-$ XGBoost the difference is $+0.021$ ($z=1.93$, $p=0.054$) and both bootstrap intervals include zero: weak, borderline evidence. For RF $-$ LSTM both bootstrap intervals exclude zero by a small margin, but DeLong does not ($p=0.189$), and the block standard error (0.006) is about half of the DeLong one (0.011). With 19 clusters the block estimate is itself imprecise, and its MDE (0.017) is about half the iid one; given the limited number of clusters and the uncertainty about the dependence structure, neither estimate is treated as definitive. We therefore describe this comparison as dependent on the inferential procedure and not as established or refuted. XGBoost against the LSTM shows no evidence of a difference, and none of the three DeLong comparisons rejects the null after Holm correction over the three pairs (smallest $p=0.054$ against a first-step threshold of $0.017$). The bootstrap intervals in \Cref{tab:paired_ci} are pointwise 95\,\% intervals, are not multiplicity-adjusted, and no bootstrap $p$-values were computed for these pairs.

Pairing removes most of the sampling error shared by two models but none of the seed variance, so the seed share is larger for a difference than for one AUC: for the classical pairs it is 17--22\,\% of a single-seed difference (block 17--35\,\%) and 2--3\,\% (2--9\,\%) of a difference of 15-seed means. For the hybrids the seed is not a minor term: in the differences of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} with Random Forest, the linear control and the standalone LSTM it is 32--47\,\% for one seed per model (block 45--47\,\%) and still 6--27\,\% (block 13--33\,\%) for the 15-seed means, and 8--47\,\% (block 16--53\,\%) for the 15-seed means of the other three hybrids. The DeLong and year-block intervals condition on the fitted seeds and omit this component; the years$\times$seeds bootstrap includes it, which is why its intervals in \Cref{tab:qnn_paired} are wider than the year-block ones.

\subsection{Simulation with Known Truth}
\label{sec:simulation}

Because DeLong and the year-block bootstrap disagree on gaps near the MDE, we check both on data whose true difference is known. We simulate two scorers whose population AUC difference is known by construction or approximated on large reference samples (four independent draws of 400{,}000 rows; Monte Carlo standard error of the population difference at most 0.0002). Labels have prevalence 0.41 with year-level effects on the logit, and the 528 rows are split among 19 years as equally as possible or, in one scenario, unequally (multinomial with Dirichlet(3) shares). The scorers are binormal with noise correlation $\rho=0.9$, which reproduces the paired DeLong standard error of the classical comparisons (0.011), and their design AUCs without year effects are 0.65 and $0.65+\Delta$. Year effects enter as a prevalence shift ($\tau_\pi$), a shift shared by both scorers ($\tau_s$) and, in scenarios C--E, a shift specific to each scorer and independent between them ($\tau_m$). Scenario B has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=0.3$, C adds $\tau_m=0.3$, D is C with unequal year sizes, and E has all three equal to 0.6. The target is the pooled population difference for new years drawn with equal weight (details in \Cref{app:noise_budget}). When the design difference is 0 the scorers are exchangeable and the population difference is exactly 0. Each scenario has 500 replications; the block bootstrap uses $B=300$ (\Cref{app:noise_budget}). Rejection rates have a Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.01 near 0.05.

\Cref{tab:sim} and \Cref{fig:sim} give three results. First, with year effects shared by the two scorers (scenario B) DeLong is calibrated (type-I error 0.040, coverage 0.960), whereas the block bootstrap rejects the exact null in 7.4\,\% of replications (coverage 0.926). \subsection{Reference Baselines: Climatology and Probabilistic Skill}
\label{sec:reference}

The comparisons so far are between learned models. A stricter question is whether any of them beats a forecast that uses only the station and the calendar month. A month$\times$station climatology fitted on the training partition reaches AUC 0.680 and a Brier score of 0.2224, 8.2\,\% better than a constant forecast at the training base rate (0.2422); persistence is weaker (lag-1 precipitation, AUC 0.589). Random Forest exceeds the climatology by $+0.003$ AUC (range $-0.002$ to $+0.011$ across seeds) and no seed is significantly better under either test; XGBoost and the LSTM score below the climatology on point estimates ($-0.018$ and $-0.011$), also without significance. Two further references require no retraining. A station-only base rate fitted on the training partition reaches AUC 0.599 (Brier score 0.2392), about half of the climatology's excess over 0.5; adding the month raises the AUC by 0.080 (DeLong $p<0.001$). Fitted on the training and validation years (1938--2007), the same climatology reaches AUC 0.686 (Brier score 0.2186), and the 15-seed Random Forest mean differs from it by $-0.003$ (DeLong $p=0.83$; year-block 95\,\% CI $[-0.025,+0.016]$): the advantage of Random Forest over the training-only climatology is smaller than what the climatology itself gains from eleven more years ($+0.006$, $p=0.27$). On proper scoring (\Cref{tab:brier}; 15-seed train-only predictions, no retraining), no model improves on the climatological Brier score on average. Random Forest matches it (mean Brier score 0.2238 against 0.2224; Brier skill score $-0.006$, from $-0.013$ to $+0.001$ across seeds, with one seed of 15 marginally below the climatology; year-block 95\,\% interval of the difference $[-0.006,+0.009]$). XGBoost, the standalone LSTM, the Linear-4D control and the four variational hybrids, all trained with positive-class weighting, score 0.017--0.023 worse than the climatology (skill $-0.075$ to $-0.104$; every year-block interval excludes zero) and have higher expected calibration errors (ECE 0.120--0.137, against 0.075 for Random Forest; whether they are over- or under-confident was not analyzed). The canonical single-run evaluation, including logistic regression, gradient boosting and the quantum-kernel SVM, gives the same picture (\Cref{S-sec:single_run,S-sec:calibration_and_block_bootstrap}).

\subsection{Label Validity under Incomplete Months}
\label{sec:labels}

Finally, we check whether the conclusions depend on how the labels are defined. Monthly precipitation is the sum of the available daily values, and months with at least 10 valid days are retained, so an incomplete month can only under-count precipitation and some months labeled negative may in fact exceed the threshold. Incomplete months are 23.3\,\% of the test partition (123 of 528; 7.5\,\% in training), and the inner join across the five daily files also discards observed rain in 121 station-months, mostly in Barcelona. We therefore re-evaluated the saved 15-seed predictions, without retraining, under eight label scenarios (S0--S7): subsets of rows with the project labels, labels from a proportional-coverage sum, and labels from the unjoined precipitation sum (full audit and tables in \Cref{S-app:labels_full}). The sign of every paired difference is preserved in all scenarios, but not its nominal significance. RF $-$ XGBoost ranges from $+0.014$ to $+0.026$ (DeLong $p$ 0.019--0.285), RF $-$ LSTM from $+0.005$ to $+0.015$ ($p$ 0.189--0.622; its year-block interval excludes zero only under the original labels, and narrowly), and RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} from $+0.011$ to $+0.020$ ($p$ 0.074--0.367; year-block interval excluding zero in five of the eight scenarios). The differences of the hybrid with the circuit-free control and with the standalone LSTM have intervals that include zero in every scenario.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What a Benchmark of This Size Can and Cannot Conclude}

Under the iid DeLong approximation the 80\,\%-power MDE for the classical seed-mean comparisons on 528 correlated test months is 0.031--0.044 AUC, and the observed gaps between the architectures studied (0.003--0.03) are mostly below it. This does not mean that the models are equal. Three observations follow. First, on the scale of one AUC, test-sampling variability exceeds seed variability for the classical models (seeds are 2--7\,\% of the combined variance) and remains the larger term for the hybrids, but there the seed share rises to 15--34\,\%, so seed and sampling error must both be reported for variational models. Second, near the MDE the verdict depends on the estimand and on the procedure: single-seed verdicts depend on which seeds are compared, and for the seed means DeLong and the cluster procedures disagree on RF $-$ LSTM and on RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN}. Third, the reference points are strong: Random Forest matches the climatology in AUC and Brier score, the other models are worse on proper scoring, and within a station no model discriminates better than the month$\times$station climatology.

For the quantum results, the four hybrids lie 0.020--0.026 AUC below Random Forest, are not distinguished from a circuit-free classical control at the same interface, and \texttt{LSTM+QTL} scores 0.012 below its own frozen classical extractor. The evidence does not establish a quantum performance advantage in this case study; it neither shows nor rules out one on larger or richer data.

\subsection{What These Results Do Not Show}
\label{sec:not_show}

Because Random Forest has the highest mean AUC, the results could be read as one more instance of tree ensembles outperforming other models on tabular data \citep{grinsztajn2022}. They do not support that reading. The gap between Random Forest and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} is resolved by the cluster procedures but not by DeLong, and it does not survive multiplicity correction; the gaps to the other three hybrids are secondary comparisons that were not adjusted for multiplicity; among the classical models, Random Forest against XGBoost is borderline and Random Forest against the LSTM depends on the procedure. Random Forest does not improve on a month$\times$station climatology in AUC or in Brier score, and once differences between stations are removed it discriminates no better than that climatology (\Cref{tab:within}). What the data support is that none of the learned models, classical or quantum, adds resolvable skill beyond the seasonal and geographic base rate of this panel, and that the differences between them are below what 528 test months can resolve.

Nor do the results show that variational circuits are ineffective in general: the study concerns 4-qubit circuits behind a 4-dimensional classical bottleneck, simulated without noise, on a small panel whose predictable signal is largely seasonal. And they do not show that DeLong or the block bootstrap is wrong: each is calibrated under some of the simulated mechanisms and not under others, and the panel does not identify which mechanism holds.

\subsection{A Low-Signal Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

The strongest empirical result of the case study is negative for every model: no learned model improves on the month$\times$station climatology in AUC or Brier score, and once differences between stations are removed all of them lose discrimination while the climatology loses little (\Cref{tab:brier,tab:within}). At a one-month lead, with monthly aggregates from four stations, the signal available beyond the seasonal and geographic base rate is therefore close to zero. Several factors may contribute: the limited predictability of monthly precipitation one month ahead from local antecedent data, monthly sums that do not resolve the convective episodes that produce much of the heavy precipitation in this region, and incomplete-month labels. The label scenarios of \Cref{sec:labels} preserve the signs of the differences, so the conclusions are not driven by the last factor, but the panel does not allow the three to be separated.

This makes the panel a poor testbed for deciding which architecture is better: on these data the answer is none of them, and a ranking of the learned models would reflect differences of a few thousandths to hundredths of AUC around a common ceiling set by the climatology. For the question of this paper it is a representative case rather than a weakness. Small QML benchmarks typically compare models whose differences are of this size, and the noise budget, the disagreement between procedures and the resolution rule describe what such a benchmark can conclude whatever the reason for the small differences. The statistical argument does not depend on the panel having little signal; the conclusions about particular architectures do, and they should be tested on data where learned models clearly beat a base-rate reference.

\subsection{Implications for Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:design}

The resolution limits above follow from the size and composition of the test set, not from the climate application, so they can be estimated before a benchmark is run.

For a binary classifier with AUC $A$, $n_1$ positives and $n_0$ negatives, the Hanley--McNeil approximation \citep{hanley1982} gives the standard error of one AUC from $(A,n_1,n_0)$ alone; for the present test set it returns 0.024, matching the DeLong value. Assuming a correlation of 0.75 between the AUCs of two models scored on the same rows (the paired standard errors measured here imply 0.72--0.83), \Cref{tab:hm_mde} gives the approximate 80\,\%-power MDE for other test-set sizes: about 0.049 for $N=500$ at prevalence 0.41 and about 0.086 at prevalence 0.10. These values are specific to the assumed AUC (0.68), inter-model correlation and iid approximation; an MDE is a design sensitivity and not a bound on detectable effects, and temporal and spatial dependence can change it in either direction for paired comparisons (for RF $-$ LSTM the block standard error here is smaller than the DeLong one). They do not replace validation on further datasets.

An empirical version of this scaling is obtained from the panel itself (\Cref{tab:resolution}; \path{exp24_resolution_curve.py}, no retraining): random subsets of 5 to 16 of the 19 test years (300 per size, all stations of a year together) are re-evaluated with the archived predictions. For every pair and every procedure the standard error falls approximately as $N^{-1/2}$ (fitted exponents 0.46--0.53), and the single-run MDEs lie about 10--20\,\% below the Hanley--McNeil values, so \Cref{tab:hm_mde} is slightly conservative here. Extrapolating with this scaling, the MDE of the 15-seed means would equal the observed Random Forest $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} gap (0.020) at about 1{,}300 test rows under DeLong, 2.5 times the present test set, but already at about 420 rows under the year jackknife, so the two procedures also disagree on how much data the comparison needs. The comparisons that bear on a quantum contribution are much further from resolution: the hybrid against the circuit-free control ($-0.009$) would need about 2{,}800--3{,}500 rows and the hybrid against the standalone LSTM ($+0.005$) about 3{,}300--4{,}400.

In practice, the MDE of a planned comparison can be computed from the test size, the prevalence and an assumed AUC before any model is trained (\Cref{tab:hm_mde}) and checked afterwards on the archived predictions (\Cref{tab:resolution}). If the differences of interest are below it, more seeds do not help: averaging seeds reduces the seed component but leaves the sampling error unchanged, and a larger test set is the only remedy. For the comparison that bears on a quantum contribution here, a hybrid against a circuit-free control at the same interface, resolving a difference of 0.01 AUC would need several thousand test cases.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Inferential procedures.} The block bootstrap relies on 19 calendar years, and for RF $-$ LSTM and RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} its standard error is about half the DeLong one; a delete-one-year jackknife agrees with it (0.0062 for both comparisons), and blocks of two to four years change only the RF $-$ XGBoost verdict. With so few clusters, cluster-based variance estimates are biased downward \citep{cameron2008}, consistent with the liberal block bootstrap in the simulations, and a block standard error below the iid value can arise from negative within-year correlation of the paired-difference components or from the downward bias of cluster-bootstrap variance estimates; it is not evidence of variance reduction, and the simulations do not establish which procedure is calibrated for the panel (\Cref{S-app:inference_details}). Small-sample corrections for clustered inference, such as the wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008}, were not applied; a test period with more independent years, or several independent test periods, would be needed to settle the RF $-$ LSTM and RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} verdicts.

\textbf{Hybrid models.} The comparisons concern 4-qubit circuits behind a 4-dimensional classical bottleneck in noiseless simulation; the hybrids have about a quarter of the trainable parameters of the standalone LSTM, so only the comparison with the Linear-4D control is capacity-matched, and the penalty of \texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN} is a hand-set heuristic rather than a physical constraint; the analyses of the three hybrids other than \texttt{LSTM+QNN} were added after the main analysis, and no equivalence margin was specified in advance. An earlier execution of the core experiment with a preprocessing error (two features misaligned across stations) gave RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} $=+0.029$ instead of $+0.020$; the change is smaller than its standard error but shows how much a seed-averaged hybrid comparison can move with a preprocessing detail (\Cref{S-sec:core_spec}).

\textbf{Label validity.} Incomplete months are 23\,\% of the test partition (\Cref{sec:labels}). The label scenarios re-evaluate fixed predictors: the models were not retrained under alternative label definitions, months with incomplete coverage among the 12 antecedent months of a window were not examined, and 897 station-months with at least 10 observed precipitation days that do not survive the inner join were not examined.

\textbf{Baseline tuning and scope.} Hyperparameters were set manually and no reproducible tuning history is available (\Cref{S-app:hyperparams}); an Optuna-tuned Random Forest on 2{,}273 rows ($0.6798\pm0.0045$) scores slightly below the untuned one on the same rows, so tuning moves the AUC by less than the noise, but XGBoost (0.662) should not be read as a ceiling for boosted trees. The panel is small (3{,}363 station-months), monthly aggregation does not resolve convective episodes, and the chronological split falls in a period of accelerated warming, so with one chronological split we cannot determine how distribution shift modifies model performance or sensitivity to optimization randomness. We evaluate one ansatz family in noiseless simulation with a 0.9--3.9\,\% quantum share of trainable parameters; inference-time gate noise changes the results little (\Cref{S-app:noise}) but says nothing about noise during training \citep{wang2021noise}.

\textbf{Generality.} All empirical conclusions concern one complete-variable panel of four stations. The sampling argument and the resolution rule transfer to other benchmarks (\Cref{sec:design}), but the findings about particular models and references do not replace validation on further datasets, preferably ones in which learned models clearly improve on a base-rate reference (\Cref{sec:testbed}).

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% ============================================================

On a benchmark of 528 correlated test station-months, the test sample, not the training seed, limits what can be concluded about the classical models: one AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, the minimum detectable difference of 15-seed means is 0.031--0.044 AUC, and it falls only as $N^{-1/2}$ with the size of the test set. For variational hybrids the seed variance is also substantial, so both sources must be reported. Near this limit the verdict depends on the procedure: DeLong and the year-block bootstrap disagree on Random Forest against the LSTM and against \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, and known-truth simulations show that each can be miscalibrated under plausible dependence between test years.

In the quantum case study, four hybrid models fitted on the same rows with 15 seeds lie 0.020--0.026 AUC below Random Forest and are not distinguished from a parameter-matched, circuit-free classical control, the only comparison that isolates the circuit. Resolving the Random Forest--hybrid gap reliably would need about 2.5 times the present test set under DeLong, and resolving whether the circuit adds anything over its control five to seven times. No model, classical or quantum, improves on a month$\times$station climatology, and within stations the climatology discriminates best. Alternative labels for incomplete months preserve the signs of the differences but not their significance.

The consequence for QML benchmarking is practical: state the estimand, compute the minimum detectable difference before ranking architectures, report seed and sampling error separately, compare against a base-rate reference and a classical control at the same interface, and treat differences below the resolution of the design as unresolved rather than as evidence for either model.

\section{Computations}
\label{app:noise_budget}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Estimators.} For two models scored on the same 528 rows, the DeLong estimator gives the covariance of the two AUCs from their structural components \citep{delong1988}; it treats station-months as independent. The block bootstrap resamples the 19 calendar years (2008--2026) with replacement, keeping all stations of a year together, recomputes both AUCs on each resample ($B=2{,}000$) and takes the standard deviation of the paired difference as its standard error \citep{kunsch1989}. With 19 clusters the estimate of the standard error is imprecise, and the direction of its discrepancy with the DeLong value depends also on the dependence structure (here the block standard error is below the DeLong value for RF $-$ LSTM: 0.010 versus 0.014 for single seeds, 0.006 versus 0.011 for seed means). The two-way bootstrap draws a calendar-year resample and, independently for each model, a resample of its 15 seeds, and recomputes the difference of seed-averaged AUCs.

\textbf{Seed-mean statistic.} The AUC of one model is the mean of the DeLong structural components over positives and over negatives, so the mean of the 15 AUCs equals the AUC statistic built from components averaged over seeds, and the paired DeLong variance of $\hat\Delta$ follows from the averaged components. This treats the 15 seeds as fixed and so conditions on the fitted models; the two-way bootstrap relaxes this by resampling seeds.

\textbf{Seed share of variance.} For model $m$ with seed standard deviation $s_m$ and sampling standard error $\text{SE}_m$, the share is $s_m^2/(s_m^2+\text{SE}_m^2)$. Using the mean DeLong SE over the 15 seeds, Random Forest (SD $0.0035$, SE $0.0232$), XGBoost ($0.0054$, $0.0236$) and the LSTM ($0.0063$, $0.0237$) give 2.3\,\%, 4.9\,\% and 6.6\,\% (2\,\%, 5\,\% and 7\,\% in \Cref{tab:budget}). For the hybrids of the core experiment, \texttt{LSTM+QNN} (SD $0.0098$, SE $0.0237$), \texttt{LbL-QNN} ($0.0129$, $0.0238$), \texttt{LSTM+QTL} ($0.0147$, $0.0239$) and \texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN} ($0.0170$, $0.0238$) give 14.7\,\%, 22.7\,\%, 27.4\,\% and 33.9\,\%.

\textbf{Simulation mechanism.} For each replication, years $u=1,\dots,19$ receive a prevalence effect $\tau_\pi\,\eta_u$ on the logit of $\pi=0.41$ and a number of rows of 528 split as equally as possible (or, in the unequal scenario, a multinomial split with Dirichlet$(3)$ shares). Scorers $k\in\{A,B\}$ are $s_{k}=\mu_k y+e_k+\tau_s\,w_u+\tau_m\,v_{k,u}$, with $e_k=\sqrt\rho\,z+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_k$ (so that the scorer noise has correlation $\rho$), $w_u$ a year shift shared by both scorers and $v_{k,u}$ a year shift specific to each scorer; all $\eta_u,w_u,v_{k,u},z,\varepsilon_k$ are standard normal and $\mu_k=\sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{AUC}_k)$ without year effects. The design AUCs without year effects are 0.65 for scorer B and $0.65+\Delta$ for scorer A; the scorer noise has correlation $\rho=0.9$, and the scorer-specific year shifts $v_{k,u}$ are independent between scorers. Scenario B has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=0.3$ and $\tau_m=0$; C adds $\tau_m=0.3$; D is C with Dirichlet year sizes; E has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=\tau_m=0.6$. The simulation target is the pooled population difference under the mechanism, with years drawn independently and weighted equally, that is, the difference for new years; in scenario D the shares of the 528 rows among years follow a Dirichlet(3) draw that is independent of the year effects, the sample statistic pools observations (so it weights years by their sizes), and the reference is the pooled population AUC with equally likely years of equal size. Scenario D changes the distribution of cluster sizes. Because the sizes are independent of the year effects, the pooled statistic is expected to be centered on the equal-weight reference; this was checked empirically and not proved: in D the mean of the estimates differs from the reference by $+0.0002$, $-0.0008$ and $-0.0004$ for design differences of 0, 0.015 and 0.03 (Monte Carlo standard error of the mean about 0.0008). The population AUC of each scorer is computed as the mean of four independent draws of 400{,}000 rows from the same mechanism with years drawn independently and equally weighted, and the Monte Carlo standard error of the population difference is reported. When the design difference is 0 the scorers are exchangeable and the population difference is set to 0. DeLong tests use the paired estimator of the seed-free case; the block bootstrap resamples years ($B=300$ per replication).
