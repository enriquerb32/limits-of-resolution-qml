\begin{abstract}
Small machine-learning benchmarks, including most applied quantum machine learning (QML) studies, rank models by AUC differences of a few hundredths. This paper asks how large a difference such a benchmark can resolve, and with which inference. The case study forecasts wet months one month ahead at four Western Mediterranean stations (529 test station-months) with classical models and two hybrids in which a classical network feeds a 4-qubit circuit. One AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, larger than the seed variability of the classical models, and the nominal minimum detectable difference between 15-seed means is 0.022--0.032 AUC. Station-months of the same year are dependent, so the primary inference is a cluster-robust procedure chosen by calibration on the test design. DeLong, which assumes independence, disagrees with it near the limit, and on an external benchmark with spatial clusters its intervals covered the reference difference in only about half of small test sets. No learned model beat a month-by-station climatology or added information to the ECMWF SEAS5 forecast, and no advantage of the circuit over classical controls at its interface was detected. We derive a reporting protocol from these results and release it as a tool.

\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Benchmarking, Statistical Power, Minimum Detectable Effect, Cluster-Robust Inference, Seed Variance, Quantum Machine Learning, Precipitation Forecasting.
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Many comparisons in machine learning are decided by a difference of a few hundredths of AUC on a few hundred test cases. Whether such a difference means anything is mostly a property of the evaluation, not of the models: it depends on how many test cases there are, on how dependent they are, and on the inferential procedure used to compare them. \textbf{The objective of this paper is to make that dependence explicit and usable}: to measure what a small benchmark with clustered test cases can resolve, to choose and validate the inference for it on the design itself, and to turn the result into a reporting protocol that needs only archived predictions. Quantum machine learning (QML) is the case study, because small benchmarks and single-run comparisons are common there and because a claimed quantum contribution can be checked against classical controls.

Claims of quantum utility on classical tabular data frequently rest on a single training run per model, capacity-asymmetric classical baselines, and uncertainty estimates that cover only one source of error \citep{bowles2024}. The machine-learning literature has long pointed out that a single run confounds initialization luck with architectural merit \citep{henderson2018,bouthillier2021}, and that tests computed on one fixed test set ignore the variance induced by the training procedure \citep{dietterich1998,nadeau2003}. Variational circuits aggravate the issue: in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era \citep{preskill2018}, parameterized quantum circuits are sensitive to initialization even at a few qubits, where barren plateaus \citep{mcclean2018} are not expected and local minima and the periodicity of the encoding matter more, and they are usually combined with a classical backbone that compresses the input into a few qubits \citep{huang2021,schuld2022}.

Throughout, ``noise'' means statistical variability (test-set sampling error and optimization-seed variance), not quantum hardware noise. The question comes before any ranking: how large a difference in AUC can a benchmark with a few hundred test cases resolve, and how much does the verdict on a small difference depend on the inferential procedure? The standard error of one AUC is set by the size and prevalence of the test set (about 0.024 for the 529 test cases studied here); it is several times the variation between training seeds of the classical models, and it falls only as $N^{-1/2}$, so repeating training with more seeds does not remove it. For differences of a few hundredths, the paired standard error, the dependence between test cases and the choice of procedure decide the verdict. Variational hybrids add a second complication: their seed variance is large enough that seed and sampling error must both be reported. The quantum case study is deliberately small: two hybrid models in which a classical network compresses the inputs to four angles that feed a 4-qubit variational circuit, simulated without noise.

We work through these questions on a deliberately modest but realistic case: a multi-decadal, multi-station Western Mediterranean precipitation panel, where monthly totals are highly variable because much of the precipitation falls in a few episodes, many linked to cut-off lows aloft (DANAs; \citealp{romero1999,nieto2005}), and where tree ensembles are strong baselines on engineered tabular features \citep{grinsztajn2022}. The task is a 1-month-ahead probabilistic forecast of a wet month, defined as monthly precipitation exceeding a pooled regional 60th percentile, from antecedent 12-month features; the moderate threshold keeps the classes balanced, and heavy-precipitation targets are not studied. The panel has the features that make small benchmarks hard to read: a few hundred correlated test cases, strong base-rate structure across sites and seasons, and incomplete records. It is a testbed for evaluation methodology, not an operational early-warning system.

Applied climate- and weather-QML studies have addressed separate parts of the problem: opportunities for climate modeling \citep{schwabe2025op}, parameter-count and initialization-matched comparisons for cloud cover \citep{schwabe2025,pastori2026}, wind-speed forecasting \citep{dasilva2026}, quantum kernels for climate emulation \citep{bazgir2024}, synthetic time-series benchmarks \citep{fellner2026}, variational data assimilation \citep{kumar2026}, and parameter-matched neural comparisons \citep{marchisio2026}. In the time-series and weather QML studies cited above, quantum models are reported as better than, or as having the potential to outperform, classical baselines on the basis of point estimates: \citet{marchisio2026} report a 15.4\,\% lower MSE than a parameter-matched classical leaky-integrate-and-fire neuron on a 2{,}000-sample test set, with one value per model and no interval or test, and \citet{dasilva2026} use 10 runs per configuration and show their spread in violin plots, but compare the models through single error values with no interval or test, although their test sets span only 14 and 5 days. We do not claim that these effects are spurious; the point is that the evidence reported cannot separate them from sampling and seed noise.

A second line of work asks what a variational circuit can represent. A circuit that encodes its inputs as rotation angles computes a truncated Fourier series of them, with frequencies fixed by the encoding gates \citep{schuld2021fourier}; such re-uploading models admit classical surrogates \citep{schreiber2023}, random Fourier features approximate many of them efficiently \citep{landman2023}, and necessary and sufficient conditions for this dequantization are known for regression \citep{sweke2025}. More broadly, the structures that make variational models trainable often make them classically simulable \citep{cerezo2025}, quantum kernels concentrate exponentially with the number of qubits \citep{thanasilp2024}, and models that are both trainable and not dequantizable exist but have to be constructed for the purpose \citep{gilfuster2025}. A classical model with the spectrum of the circuit is therefore the natural control for any claimed quantum contribution. Applied benchmarks rarely include it; the case study below does, at the same interface and on the same rows, and also emulates the trained circuit exactly and plants circuit-generated signals to see how often a trained circuit is distinguished from its controls.

\textbf{Where this paper sits.} Three lines of work bear on the question: the variance of machine-learning benchmarks, where the training seed, the split and the test sample each move results by amounts comparable to reported improvements, and comparisons in NLP, neuroimaging and language-model evaluation have been shown to be underpowered or to ignore clustered errors \citep{card2020,varoquaux2018,miller2024}; systematic QML benchmarks, in which tuned classical models are rarely beaten \citep{bowles2024}; and the theory of classical surrogates for variational circuits reviewed above. Each line answers part of the question; none gives the person running a small applied benchmark a procedure for deciding, before and after the experiment, whether its comparisons can be read at all, and whether a claimed quantum contribution would have been detected.

\textbf{What the paper does not claim.} It does not rank the architectures. In particular, it does not show that Random Forest, or tree ensembles in general, beat quantum or neural models on tabular data: no learned model improves on a month$\times$station climatology, and within a station the climatology has the highest AUC (\Cref{sec:not_show}). For the same reason the panel is a poor testbed for choosing an architecture; it is used here for the statistical question, for which small differences between models are the case of interest (\Cref{sec:testbed}).

\textbf{Contributions.} The statistical tools are standard; what is new is that each of them is checked on the design of the study instead of assumed, and that the checks are combined into a protocol.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A noise budget with an explicit estimand.} The estimand is the difference of seed-averaged AUCs for new years at the same stations; test-sampling and seed error are reported separately for three classical and two hybrid models on 529 test station-months (\Cref{sec:framework,sec:budget}).
    \item \textbf{Inference chosen by calibration, not by default.} Every candidate procedure is calibrated in a simulation of the real test design; CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom is the one that stays calibrated, and it becomes the primary procedure. Its disagreement with DeLong is traced to a co-movement of stations within years, and its verdicts are tested against clusters of consecutive years and against single influential years (\Cref{sec:sampling,sec:budget,sec:simulation}).
    \item \textbf{A resolution rule for benchmark design}: the minimum detectable AUC difference of two models on the same $N$ test cases falls as $N^{-1/2}$ when the added cases are independent and the covariance between models is unchanged (0.032 for 15-seed means of Random Forest against \texttt{LSTM+QNN} at $N=529$; an extrapolation of about 0.016 at $N\approx2{,}100$). Its predictions were recorded before the analysis of a second panel, and an external benchmark with real signal and spatial clusters, also designed before any fit, shows which decisions small test sets lead to (\Cref{sec:design,sec:replication,sec:external}).
    \item \textbf{Tested quantum controls.} Classical and frozen-circuit controls at the circuit's four-dimensional interface, an exact classical emulation of the trained circuits, heads trained on common frozen angles, repeated planted circuit signals with a null, finite-shot inference, and climatological and operational (ECMWF SEAS5) references (\Cref{sec:applying,sec:reference}).
    \item \textbf{A reporting protocol and a tool}: \Cref{tab:protocol}, whose inferential items are implemented in an open library that needs only archived predictions, labels, clusters and seeds (\Cref{sec:protocol}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data, Task and Models}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Dataset, Target and Split}
\label{sec:dataset_merge}

The study domain is the Spanish Mediterranean coast (Catalonia, the Valencian Community and the Region of Murcia), a transition zone between Mediterranean (\textit{Csa}) and semi-arid (\textit{BSk}/\textit{BSh}) K\"{o}ppen regimes subject to severe convective episodes \citep{romero1999,nieto2005}. Daily records of four AEMET stations, Alacant/Alicante, Barcelona Aeropuerto, Val\`{e}ncia and San Javier Aeropuerto (\Cref{tab:station_coverage}), were obtained from \textit{datosclima.es} under the AEMET Open Data framework (downloaded on 1 June 2026) and aggregated to calendar months per station.

The \emph{label} is computed from the precipitation series alone: the monthly total is defined when at most 3 days of the month are missing (trace precipitation counts as zero) and is otherwise left undefined, and no label depends on days dropped because another variable was missing. With missing days the observed total would be a lower bound of the true one, so a fixed threshold could only err towards a dry label; in this panel the case does not arise, because every window target and every training month that enters the threshold has a complete record (\Cref{S-sec:lookahead}).

The \emph{predictors} are the precipitation totals of the 12 preceding months (rescaled to the month length when at least 10 days are observed) and monthly means over the observed days of pressure at station level and its variability, temperature and diurnal range, wind speed and maximum gust, sunshine duration, and FAO-56 Penman--Monteith reference evapotranspiration \citep{allen1998} with humidity estimated from the minimum temperature, radiation from sunshine duration and wind from the observed speed ($u_2=0.748\,u_{10}$).

A covariate needs at least 10 observed days in a month, and missing monthly covariates are interpolated linearly within the station over at most two consecutive months (in a longer gap, the first two months). This interpolation, the filling of the antecedent-summer evapotranspiration and of a missing one-month evapotranspiration lag use later or full-record values, so they look ahead. A truncation test shows that no test window is affected: rebuilt from the records available at the end of month $t-1$, all 529 test windows have the same inputs and the same eligibility. The lookahead reaches 24 training and 2 validation windows, and retraining the tree ensembles, the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} on a causal panel changes their test AUC by at most 0.002 (\Cref{S-sec:lookahead}), so the results below use the archived pipeline.

Every calendar month is kept, so the windows are contiguous in time, and a window is used only if its target month has a label, its last antecedent month has all predictors and its 12 precipitation lags are defined. Inhomogeneities from relocations or instrument changes remain an accepted limitation \citep{alexandersson1986,peterson1998}.

Each window uses months $i,\dots,i+11$ (35 features: 12 precipitation lags, 17 engineered physical features, 2 cyclic month terms and 4 station indicators) to predict whether precipitation in month $i+12$ exceeds the pooled regional 60th percentile of the training labels, $P_{60}=\qty{29.9}{\milli\meter}$. The split is chronological: training 1938--1996 (2{,}332 windows), validation 1997--2007 (516) and test 2008--2026 (529; 40.6\,\% positive). The precipitation series are almost complete (0--5\,\% missing days), but the pressure records of Valencia and San Javier are not (19 and 25\,\% of days missing over their spans), and after 2012 and 2011 these two stations have no complete set of predictors; 83.4\,\% of the test windows therefore come from Alicante and Barcelona. Test base rates differ between stations (27--55\,\%), so part of the pooled AUC reflects telling stations apart (\Cref{sec:reference}). All scaling and anomaly statistics use pre-1997 data only. The test period falls in a time of accelerated warming, so the evaluation takes place under a covariate shift whose effect cannot be isolated with one chronological split.

\textbf{Revision of the data pipeline.} An earlier version of this analysis built the monthly panel from an inner join of the five daily variable files, so that a day without pressure or wind also removed its precipitation; 23\,\% of the test months were then labelled from incomplete totals. It also set missing precipitation and sunshine to zero, used a constant wind in the evapotranspiration, and joined non-contiguous months into windows. The results reported here come from the corrected pipeline, rerun from the raw files with tuned tree baselines in one pinned software environment; \Cref{S-app:v1} lists the changes and compares the headline numbers of the two versions. The test set changed from 528 to 529 windows with the same station composition, so the missing stations after 2012 are a property of the covariate records, not of the join.

\subsection{Models and Fitting Sets}
\label{sec:architectures}

\textbf{Models in the main analyses.} Two references, the ECMWF SEAS5 seasonal forecast (\Cref{sec:seas5}) and a month$\times$station climatology fitted on the training partition, are compared with three classical models, Random Forest \citep{breiman2001}, XGBoost and a standalone bidirectional LSTM (10{,}103 parameters), and with two hybrid quantum--classical models simulated without noise in PennyLane (state-vector simulator \texttt{default.qubit}, exact expectation values, gradients by backpropagation, double precision). The two tree ensembles were tuned by an automated search on the validation AUC with declared search spaces (Optuna, TPE sampler, 50 trials each): Random Forest selected 350 trees of depth 11 (minimum split 13, minimum leaf 10, $\sqrt{p}$ features), and XGBoost, which stops early on the validation AUC, a learning rate of 0.036 with trees of depth 3. Early stopping keeps very few trees: 2 to 121 across the 15 seeds (median 16, and 2--3 trees in five seeds), so the XGBoost of this paper is a small, heavily regularized ensemble rather than a typical boosted model; the search spaces, the selected values and the untuned configurations are in \Cref{S-app:hyperparams}. \texttt{LSTM+QNN} compresses a 51-dimensional vector (the 16-dimensional LSTM state concatenated with the 35 features) to 4 rotation angles $q\in[0,\pi]^4$ that feed a 4-qubit circuit of two layers, each re-uploading the angles as $R_Z(q_j)R_Y(q_j)$ ($R_Y$ acts first on $|0\rangle$, so $R_Z$ is not a global phase) before one \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers} block of range $r=1$ (a ring of nearest-neighbour CNOTs; the template is called once per layer, so both layers have $r=1$); the readout is $\langle Z_j\rangle$ on every qubit, followed by LayerNorm and a linear output layer. Because LayerNorm standardizes the four readouts of each row, the model is approximately invariant to a common contraction of the $\langle Z_j\rangle$, such as global depolarizing noise would cause. The invariance is not exact, because of the $\epsilon$ in the LayerNorm denominator, and it breaks down when the contraction makes the spread of the readouts comparable to $\sqrt{\epsilon}$. It does not extend to shot noise or to qubit-dependent noise. Each angle enters four rotation gates, so the model computes a truncated Fourier series of the angles with per-coordinate frequencies $\{0,\pm1,\dots,\pm4\}$, with products across coordinates generated by the entangling gates \citep{schuld2021fourier}; it has 2{,}613 parameters, 24 of them quantum. \texttt{LSTM+QTL} is a quantum head on a pre-trained standalone LSTM \citep{mari2020}: in each seed the LSTM of that seed is frozen in evaluation mode, and a trainable adapter ($67\to8\to4$, angles in $[0,\pi]$) feeds a 2-layer circuit with a single $R_Y$ encoding (per-coordinate frequencies $\{0,\pm1\}$; one \texttt{StronglyEntanglingLayers} call with two layers, so PennyLane's default ranges $r=1$ and $r=2$). A variant of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} trained with a hand-set penalty (\texttt{LSTM+PI-QNN}) is reported in \Cref{S-app:hybrids}.

\textbf{Controls at the interface.} Eleven classical or frozen-circuit controls isolate the circuit. The Linear-4D control has the architecture of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} with the circuit replaced by a linear $4\to4$ map (2{,}609 parameters against 2{,}613). Four separable Fourier controls apply the linear map to $[\sin kq,\cos kq]_{k\le d}$ for $d=1,\dots,4$ (\texttt{Fourier-1} to \texttt{Fourier-4}; 36--132 parameters in the map), the last one covering the circuit's full per-coordinate spectrum without its cross-coordinate products. Two tensor-product surrogates, \texttt{Full Fourier-1} and \texttt{Full Fourier-2}, use the bases $\prod_{j=1}^{4}\{1,\cos k q_j,\sin k q_j\}_{k\le d}$ for $d=1$ (81 features) and $d=2$ (625 features); a full degree-4 basis would have $9^4=6{,}561$ features for 2{,}332 training rows. Two controls keep the circuit but freeze its weights, at random values in $(-\pi,\pi)$ or at zero (the encodings and the CNOT rings remain, so the circuit is a fixed nonlinear map of the angles). For \texttt{LSTM+QTL}, \texttt{QTL-classical} replaces its circuit by a linear $4\to4$ map, and \texttt{QTL tensor-81} by a linear map on the 81 functions $\prod_j\{1,\cos q_j,\sin q_j\}$, which span exactly the readouts of a circuit with one $R_Y$ encoding per angle; it is the exact classical counterpart of that head. All controls share the training loop of the model they are compared with (\Cref{S-tab:core_spec}). Comparisons of a hybrid with the standalone LSTM, which has about four times its parameters, or with Random Forest are comparisons of whole pipelines and cannot be attributed to the circuit; the comparisons that isolate the circuit are those with the controls.

\textbf{Fitting sets, seeds and environment.} All models use the same 2{,}332 training rows; the neural and hybrid models and XGBoost use the 516 validation rows for early stopping; all are scored on the same 529 test rows, with 15 seeds each. All results come from one execution in a pinned environment (Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.15.0 CPU, PennyLane 0.45.1, scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 2.0.3; lock file with hashes in the repository; \Cref{S-tab:software_env}) with a fixed number of threads. The primary metric is the ROC-AUC, complemented by the Brier score \citep{brier1950}.

\section{Statistical Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Estimands and Inference}
\label{sec:estimand}

For models $A$ and $B$ with test probabilities $p_{A,s}$, $p_{B,s}$ from seeds $s=1,\dots,S$ ($S=15$), the main test statistic of this paper is the difference between seed-averaged test AUCs,
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta=\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{A,s})-\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{B,s}),
\end{equation}
and, for single-run comparisons, the difference of two individual AUCs. As in forecast verification, the two models are scored on the same cases, so their errors are dependent and must be compared paired \citep{diebold1995,delsole2014}. Sampling inference targets the corresponding performance difference for the evaluation population, conditional on the observed training data and on the fitted seed-specific models. Variation due to the choice of training set, which can dominate comparisons of learning algorithms \citep{nadeau2003,bouthillier2021} and is what cross-validation estimates \citep{bates2024}, is outside this estimand: the chronological split is fixed, as it is in operational use. Two populations are possible here. One is the observed panel itself: these stations, months and test years, with the station mix they happen to have (83.4\,\% of the test windows come from Alicante and Barcelona, the other two stations leave the panel after 2011--2012, and 2026 is partial). The other is new years at the same stations. Every inferential statement of this paper is about the second: the estimand is the difference of seed-averaged AUCs for new years at these stations, conditional on the training data and the fitted models; the first is described, not inferred. The DeLong variance treats the 529 station-months as independent draws. Applied to clustered data it is not a formal conditioning on the observed years; it is a reference under independence. The year-block bootstrap, the cluster-robust variance and the simulations of \Cref{sec:simulation} target the difference for new years at these stations, and they are the procedures that respect the dependence between station-months of the same year. A bootstrap that also resamples seeds targets the seed-averaged difference over the distribution of optimization seeds. The mean of AUCs is linear in the DeLong structural components, so the paired sampling variance of $\hat\Delta$ is obtained by averaging the components over seeds; this treats the seeds as fixed.

\subsection{Sampling Uncertainty}
\label{sec:sampling}

Test-set sampling error is estimated in four ways. (i) The paired DeLong estimator for correlated ROC curves \citep{delong1988,hanley1982} treats station-months as independent. (ii) A calendar-year cluster bootstrap (``year-block bootstrap'') resamples the 19 test years (2008--2026) with all stations of a year together ($B=2{,}000$; \citealp{field2007}); intervals are percentile intervals. (iii) A two-way bootstrap resamples years and seeds. The seeds are drawn jointly when seed $s$ of both models shares a fitted component (\texttt{LSTM+QTL}, \texttt{QTL-classical} and \texttt{QTL tensor-81} are heads on the frozen standalone LSTM of the same seed, which is also the extractor and the LSTM of the comparisons), so that the shared network is resampled once; otherwise they are drawn independently, since a common seed number in two separately trained models does not pair them. (iv) An analytic cluster-robust estimator \citep{obuchowski1997} sums the influence values of $\hat\Delta$ (the seed-averaged DeLong components, centered and divided by the number of positives or negatives) within each year, $\text{SE}_{\text{cl}}=\{K/(K-1)\sum_u S_u^2\}^{1/2}$ with $S_u$ the sum over the rows of year $u$; DeLong is the same sum without grouping, and the ratio $\text{SE}_{\text{cl}}/\text{SE}_{\text{DeLong}}$ (``cluster/iid'') summarizes the within-year dependence. Splitting $S_u$ into the contributions of the stations separates the cluster variance into within-station and cross-station terms, and permuting the years independently within each station gives its distribution when stations do not co-move within a year. As a check of the cluster definition, the cluster-robust interval is also computed with 12-month clusters starting in each of the 12 calendar months (``phases''), and we report for how many phases it excludes zero. With only 19 clusters, cluster-based variance estimates are biased downward \citep{cameron2008}. (v) The Bell--McCaffrey CR2 estimator \citep{bell2002} rescales each year sum, $\text{SE}_{\text{CR2}}=\{\sum_u S_u^2/(1-n_u/N)\}^{1/2}$, and refers $\hat\Delta/\text{SE}_{\text{CR2}}$ to a $t$ distribution with the Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom of the intercept-only working model under independence, which for the unequal year sizes of this test set are $\nu=15.9$ rather than 18 (\path{qml_resolution.cluster_robust_delta}). A studentized wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights \citep{webb2023} and a delete-one-year jackknife are reported as checks (\Cref{S-app:fewclusters}).

\textbf{Primary procedure.} The estimand is the difference for new years at the same stations, so the primary procedure must respect the dependence between station-months of a year. It was chosen by calibrating every candidate on a simulation of the test design (\Cref{sec:simulation}, \path{exp48_primary_calibration.py}), with the real coverage mask and with year effects on prevalence and scores, scorer-specific year$\times$station effects, a prevalence dipole between stations, AR(1) dependence between years, and test sets with 10, 19 or 38 years and two or four stations per year. With independent years, CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom covers at 0.935--0.975 in every scenario, and it is the best-calibrated procedure with 10 years. DeLong falls to 0.85 when there are year$\times$station effects, and the percentile block bootstrap falls to 0.84--0.94 and rejects a null difference in 6--13\,\% of replications. CR2 is therefore the primary procedure: its interval and $p$-value decide whether a difference is resolved, and Holm correction is applied to its $p$-values. DeLong is reported as the reference under independence, the years$\times$seeds bootstrap for the seed uncertainty that CR2, conditional on the fitted models, does not include, and the block bootstrap in the Supplement. No procedure based on calendar-year clusters is calibrated when the year effects are serially correlated (AR(1) coefficient 0.5 with year$\times$station effects: coverage 0.87--0.91 for every one); the sensitivity to clusters of two consecutive years is reported in \Cref{S-app:fewclusters}.

\subsection{Seed Variability, Minimum Detectable Difference and Scope}

For a model with seed standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{seed}}$ and mean sampling standard error $\text{SE}$ of one AUC we report the seed share $\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2/(\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2+\text{SE}^2)$, a descriptive ratio of two conditional variances, not a decomposition of the total uncertainty of a training procedure. For a paired difference with standard error $\text{SE}_\Delta$, the minimum detectable difference at two-sided $\alpha=0.05$ and power 0.80 is $\text{MDE}=(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\text{SE}_\Delta\approx2.80\,\text{SE}_\Delta$. It describes the sensitivity of the design to a true difference; it is not a threshold that an observed difference must exceed, and an observed gap between $1.96\,\text{SE}_\Delta$ and $2.80\,\text{SE}_\Delta$ can be significant.
\label{sec:multiplicity}

The primary analyses are the noise budget, the paired comparisons, the simulations and the comparison with the climatology; none was pre-registered. Holm correction is applied to the CR2 $p$-values within two families of three seed-mean comparisons (the classical models, and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} against Random Forest, the Linear-4D control and the LSTM); correcting miscalibrated $p$-values for multiplicity would not make them calibrated, which is why the correction is applied to the primary procedure. The remaining analyses, about forty paired comparisons, are secondary, unadjusted, and isolated nominal rejections among them are expected by chance. A difference is called \emph{resolved} when its CR2 interval excludes zero, and, in the two primary families, \emph{resolved after Holm} when its CR2 $p$-value passes the Holm step. Agreement or disagreement of DeLong with the primary procedure is reported as a diagnostic of the dependence between test cases, not as part of the decision. An earlier version of this analysis called a difference resolved only when DeLong, the block bootstrap and the cluster-robust $t_{18}$ interval all excluded zero; that rule had no stated error rate and is replaced.

\section{Results}
% ============================================================

The results follow the question of the paper in three steps. \Cref{sec:budget,sec:simulation,sec:design} measure what the first panel can resolve: how large the sampling and seed errors are, which procedure is calibrated for this design, and how the resolution scales with the test size. \Cref{sec:applying,sec:reference,sec:stationmonth,sec:seas5} apply that resolution to the quantum case study and to its references: the hybrids against classical pipelines and interface controls, and every learned model against the climatology and the ECMWF forecast. \Cref{sec:replication,sec:external} test the conclusions out of sample, on a second panel and on an external benchmark with real signal.

\textbf{Why the procedures disagree.} DeLong treats every test month as independent; the cluster procedures treat the months of one calendar year as a block. They give different answers only when the year blocks behave differently from independent months, and here they do so only for Random Forest against the neural and hybrid models: the cluster standard error is 59--78\,\% of the DeLong one for those pairs, and 81--142\,\% for pairs of neural or hybrid models (column ``Cluster/iid'' of the tables). A smaller cluster error means that the advantage of one model is steadier from year to year than independent months would suggest. Splitting each year's contribution by station shows why (\Cref{S-app:cancellation}): in the years in which Random Forest gains on the LSTM at one station, it tends to lose at another, so the station contributions partly cancel within each year and the yearly totals vary less. Three checks support this reading. Shuffling the years independently within each station, which breaks the link between stations and keeps everything else, brings the ratio back towards 0.75--0.87, and the observed values lie in the lower tail of that distribution ($p=0.07$ and 0.12). Removing a constant per station changes nothing, whereas removing the mean of each station, calendar month and class brings the ratio to 1.0--1.06: the cancellation comes from seasonal patterns that differ between stations and between the two model families. The co-movement is, however, concentrated in time (\path{exp45_cluster_periods.py}): restricted to 2008--2012, the only five years with all four stations, the cross-station term is $-88$\,\% and $-99$\,\% of the within-station one for RF $-$ LSTM and RF $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, and Random Forest has no advantage (gaps $-0.004$ and $-0.0005$); in 2013--2026, with two stations, the terms are $-25$\,\% and $-3$\,\%, the cluster/iid ratios 0.65 and 0.87, and the gaps $+0.023$ and $+0.030$. Because the composition of the test set changes with the period, a difference in the relative behaviour of the models between the two periods is an alternative reading of the same pattern, and the two cannot be separated with five four-station years. Removing the partial year 2026 (nine rows, January--May) leaves the ratios unchanged (0.60 and 0.73). Finally, the effect was stronger in the earlier version of the analysis, whose labels were partly wrong (\Cref{S-app:v1}), and it did not appear on the second panel (\Cref{sec:replication}). The cluster procedures are therefore not wrong on this panel, but their extra power over DeLong rests on a pattern of four stations over 19 years that need not recur. It is also a pattern that the calibration of the primary procedure does not contain: none of the simulated mechanisms, including scorer-specific year$\times$station effects and a prevalence dipole between stations, produces cluster/iid ratios as low as 0.60 or 0.72 in more than 1.2\,\% of replications (\path{exp48_primary_calibration.py}), and we could not construct one that does. The coverage of CR2 is established for the simulated mechanisms, not for this one, so the primary verdicts on Random Forest against the neural models are the least secure of the paper. They also depend on treating calendar years as independent: with clusters of two consecutive years they are no longer resolved ($p=0.061$ and 0.051), and leaving out a single test year reverses the verdict on Random Forest against the LSTM for 9 of the 19 years. The effect of freezing the circuit holds for blocks of one to three years and for every omitted year (\Cref{S-app:fewclusters}). Standard small-sample corrections for 19 clusters do not change them (\Cref{S-tab:fewclusters}).

\subsection{Simulation with Known Truth}
\label{sec:simulation}

Because DeLong and the cluster procedures disagree on gaps near the MDE, we check both on data whose true difference is known. We simulate two scorers whose population AUC difference is known by construction or approximated on large reference samples. Labels have prevalence 0.41 with year-level effects on the logit, and 528 rows are split among 19 years; these are the size and prevalence of the test set of the first version of the analysis (\Cref{S-app:v1}), kept because the mechanism does not use the data, and the version matched to the present test set (529 rows, prevalence 0.406) is reported at the end of this section. The scorers are binormal with noise correlation $\rho=0.9$, which reproduces the paired DeLong standard error of the classical comparisons (0.011), and their design AUCs without year effects are 0.65 and $0.65+\Delta$. Year effects enter as a prevalence shift ($\tau_\pi$), a shift shared by both scorers ($\tau_s$) and, in scenarios C--E, a shift specific to each scorer ($\tau_m$). Scenario B has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=0.3$, C adds $\tau_m=0.3$, D is C with unequal year sizes, and E has all three equal to 0.6 (details in \Cref{app:noise_budget}). Each scenario has 500 replications.

The simulations (\Cref{S-tab:sim,S-fig:sim}) give three results. First, with year effects shared by the two scorers (scenario B) DeLong is calibrated (type-I error 0.040, coverage 0.960), whereas the block bootstrap rejects the exact null in 7.4\,\% of replications. Second, with scorer-specific year effects the DeLong standard error is too small (ratio of Monte Carlo SD to mean SE 1.07, 1.17 and 1.57 in scenarios C--E; type-I error 0.068, 0.096 and 0.220), whereas the block standard error tracks the SD (1.01--1.08) with a type-I error of 0.074--0.084: neither procedure is uniformly better calibrated. Third, where DeLong is calibrated power follows the MDE rule: in scenario B (MDE 0.031) a true difference of 0.029 is detected in 74\,\% of replications and 0.0145 in 32\,\%. A version calibrated to the year sizes, prevalence and score correlation of the panel gives type-I errors of 0.048--0.058 for DeLong and 0.064--0.088 for the block bootstrap, and only 17--24\,\% power at an observed gap of 0.013 (\Cref{S-app:panel_matched}). None of these mechanisms produces a cluster standard error well below the DeLong one; that pattern needs the cross-station co-movement described in \Cref{sec:budget}. These scenarios compare DeLong and the block bootstrap; the wider calibration on the real coverage mask that selects the primary procedure, with every candidate procedure, is summarized in \Cref{sec:sampling} and reported in \Cref{S-app:fewclusters}.

\subsection{Resolution and Test-Set Size}
\label{sec:design}

The resolution limits of \Cref{sec:budget} follow from the size and composition of the test set, not from the climate application, so they can be estimated before a benchmark is run. For a binary classifier with AUC $A$, $n_1$ positives and $n_0$ negatives, the Hanley--McNeil approximation \citep{hanley1982} gives the standard error of one AUC from $(A,n_1,n_0)$ alone; for the present test set it returns 0.024, matching the DeLong value. For two models scored on the same rows, the standard error of the difference follows from the two standard errors and the correlation between the AUCs \citep{hanley1983}. Assuming a correlation of 0.75, \Cref{tab:hm_mde} gives the approximate 80\,\%-power MDE for other test-set sizes: about 0.049 for $N=500$ at prevalence 0.41 and about 0.086 at prevalence 0.10. These values are specific to the assumed AUC (0.68), inter-model correlation and iid approximation; temporal and spatial dependence can change them in either direction for paired comparisons. The correlation of 0.75 is conservative for this panel: the DeLong correlation of two single-run AUCs (median over seeds; \path{exp46_auc_correlation.py}) is 0.78 for Random Forest and \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, 0.86 for Random Forest and the LSTM and 0.91 for the two tree ensembles, and with these values the approximation gives 0.045 and 0.036 for the first two pairs, against empirical MDEs of 0.046 and 0.035 (\Cref{fig:resolution}). The correlation $\rho=0.89$ of the panel-matched simulation (\Cref{S-app:panel_matched}) is a different quantity, the correlation of the two scorers' normal scores.

\textbf{Does training the circuit help, and does it help because it is quantum?} \Cref{tab:controls} answers the two questions separately. Freezing the circuit at random or zero weights lowers the AUC by 0.012--0.013, a difference resolved by the primary procedure (CR2 $p=0.010$--0.014) with which DeLong agrees; only the years$\times$seeds interval, which also includes the larger seed variation of the frozen models, reaches zero. Training the circuit therefore helps, for the fitted seeds. Whether it helps because the map is quantum is answered by the Fourier surrogates, classical maps that apply sines and cosines of the same four angles: all of them score as well as the trained circuit or slightly better, and no difference is resolved (\Cref{tab:controls}; the separable Fourier-1, -3 and -4 maps in \Cref{S-tab:controls_more}). This is the empirical counterpart of the classical-surrogate argument \citep{schreiber2023,landman2023}, with a different control: the surrogates here are separate models trained end to end like the hybrid, whereas those of \citet{schreiber2023} are derived from a given circuit.

\textbf{What the circuit computes.} One reason no advantage is detected is that, on the angles it actually receives, the trained circuit computes something simple. On the training and test angles, its four readouts before the LayerNorm are close to linear combinations of the 81 products $\prod_j\{1,\cos q_j,\sin q_j\}$ (mean out-of-sample $R^2=0.984$ over the 15 circuits), although the circuit could produce frequencies up to 4 in each angle. This describes the readouts on the visited angles, not the whole torus and not the head after the LayerNorm. Circuits with random weights are almost as simple on the same angles ($R^2=0.974$), so this is a property of the encoding on the range of angles that the model visits, not something that training discovers (\Cref{S-app:spectrum}).

\textbf{Emulation, substitution and learning, separated.} The end-to-end controls share the interface dimension with the hybrid but not its learned angles, so they mix three questions (\path{exp52_emulation_substitution.py}, \Cref{S-app:emulation}). \emph{Emulation}: each readout of the trained circuit is a trigonometric polynomial with frequencies up to 4 per angle, so its values on a grid of $9^4$ angles determine it exactly; for all 15 circuits the reconstructed polynomials, pushed through the same LayerNorm and output layer, reproduce the logits to $3\times10^{-14}$ and the AUC exactly. Truncating that exact series to frequency 1 per angle costs 0.058 AUC on average, whereas a least-squares degree-1 fit on the visited angles loses almost nothing: the circuit is simple on the angles it receives, not on the whole torus. \emph{Substitution and learning on common angles}: with the LSTM and projection of each seed frozen, every head receives exactly the same angles. A circuit trained from scratch on them (15-seed mean AUC 0.652) is below the separable Fourier-2 map (0.664; CR2 $p=0.002$) and the 81- and 625-function tensor maps (0.668 and 0.667; $p=0.001$ and 0.013), and not distinguished from Linear-4D (0.654); the original end-to-end circuit (0.660) is not distinguished from Fourier-2 and lies 0.008 below the 81-function map (CR2 $p=0.035$; the years$\times$seeds interval reaches zero). The circuit can thus be emulated exactly at a cost of 6{,}561 coefficients per readout, a classical surrogate learned on the same angles generalizes at least as well, and the circuit itself is the harder map to learn.

\textbf{Would the controls detect a circuit contribution?} With no learned model above the climatology, the absence of a detected circuit advantage could hold simply because there is nothing for the circuit to learn. We therefore planted a signal generated by a circuit (\Cref{S-app:teacher}). A ``teacher'' circuit with the architecture of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} and fixed random weights scored the real inputs, and new labels were drawn from its score; the teacher was chosen so that most of its signal (89\,\%) lies in interactions between angles that the separable surrogates cannot represent. \Cref{tab:teacher_main} shows the result when every head receives the teacher's angles. For this teacher the trained circuit beats the linear and separable maps, and the classical tensor-product surrogates recover the signal almost completely, as the dequantization results predict for a circuit of this size. One teacher, chosen among 20 for its non-separable signal, with one draw of labels and five seeds, is a stress test, not evidence of power, and its score had no LayerNorm while every student head has one. We therefore repeated the control (\path{exp50_teacher_grid.py}, \Cref{S-app:teacher}) with four teachers not selected for anything, scored through the same LayerNorm as the students, at two signal strengths (oracle AUC 0.70 and 0.85), with a second label draw for one teacher (10 configurations), and with two null configurations whose labels do not depend on any teacher. With the primary procedure on the paired seed means, the trained circuit is above the Linear-4D map in 6 of the 10 configurations, never above the separable Fourier-2 map (which is above it in 2), and below the 81-function tensor surrogate in all 10; no contrast rejects under the null. The tensor surrogates detect a planted circuit signal in every configuration, but a trained circuit head does not learn a circuit-generated function better than its classical surrogates, and its advantage over the separable maps in \Cref{tab:teacher_main} does not generalize across teachers. When complete models must also learn the angles from the raw inputs, no model recovers the planted signal (AUC 0.59--0.62 for every model, including the hybrid; \Cref{S-tab:teacher}): a comparison of complete models on this panel would not detect a circuit contribution of this kind, which is why the controls at the interface are the relevant ones.

\subsection{Skill beyond the Seasonal and Station Base Rates}
\label{sec:stationmonth}

Pooling cases with different base rates credits a forecast with skill it does not have \citep{hamill2006}. Defining the target as exceedance of the training 60th percentile of the same station and calendar month removes that signal from the training labels. It does not remove it from the test labels by construction: cells are small, totals tie at zero in dry months, and the test years may be wetter or drier than the training years in a given season. Under this label no model discriminates (\Cref{tab:stationmonth}): the 15-seed mean AUCs lie between 0.505 and 0.514, against 0.479 for the climatology, and no paired difference is distinguished from zero (DeLong $p\ge0.70$). After Platt recalibration every model has a lower Brier score than the raw climatology, but with AUCs near 0.5 this is a correction of the base rate towards that of the validation years; against the climatology recalibrated with the same map the skill is $+0.004$ to $+0.006$, and the year-block interval of the Brier difference includes zero for every model. The same conclusion follows without changing the label. With the original pooled label, an AUC computed only over positive--negative pairs of the same station and calendar month gives the climatology exactly 0.5, because it is constant within a cell, so for this AUC the contrast against 0.5 is also the contrast against the climatology (for the pooled AUC the two are different: 0.5 against 0.685). Every model scores 0.449--0.495 (year-block intervals include 0.5 for four of the five models; that of \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, $[0.423,0.497]$, lies just below; \path{exp49_review_minor.py}). The predictors therefore carry no detectable information about monthly exceedance beyond the seasonal cycle and the station, and the AUCs of 0.65--0.68 of \Cref{tab:case} are consistent with the models reproducing those base rates.

\subsection{An Operational Reference: ECMWF SEAS5}
\label{sec:seas5}

The climatology is the minimal reference; an agency would compare a statistical model with its dynamical seasonal forecast. We therefore add ECMWF SEAS5 \citep{johnson2019seas5}, distributed by the Copernicus Climate Change Service \citep{c3s_seasonal_monthly}, as a further model in the same protocol (\Cref{S-app:seas5}). Monthly precipitation of the first 25 members on the $1^\circ$ grid is interpolated bilinearly to each station. For lead 1 the forecast for month $M$ starts on the first day of $M$; it is released several days into $M$ (on the 5th to ECMWF Member States and on the 6th--13th through C3S, depending on the dataset and the period; \citealp{c3s_seasonal_monthly}), whereas the statistical models are available on the first day. Lead 2 (start on the first day of $M-1$) is available before $M$ begins and is the operational comparison at a common issue date; lead 1 is a reference issued later, not a demonstrated upper bound. The probability of exceedance, specified before the analysis, is the fraction of members above the model's own quantile at the level $1-p_{\text{clim}}$, with $p_{\text{clim}}$ the training exceedance frequency of the station and calendar month and the model quantile taken from the hindcasts of 1981--1996, so that no test or validation information enters the bias correction. Member fractions of a finite ensemble are not fair scores in the sense of \citet{ferro2014}; probabilistic scores use SEAS5 after Platt recalibration on the validation partition. To ask whether the statistical models add information to the dynamical forecast, we fit for each model and seed, on the validation partition, a logistic combination of the logits of the model and of SEAS5, and compare it with the same combination of the climatology and SEAS5.

\subsection{External Benchmark with Real Signal}
\label{sec:external}

Both panels have little signal, so we asked what the protocol decides when the models do discriminate. The design, the contrasts, the margin of relevance and five predictions were recorded and committed before any model was fitted (\path{audit/prediction_external.txt}). The data are the 20{,}640 block groups of California Housing \citep{pace1997}; the target is a median house value above the training median, the clusters are cells of $0.25^\circ\times0.25^\circ$, and the split is by cell (259 training, 77 validation and 182 test cells; 10{,}282 test rows). Logistic regression, Random Forest, XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron (5 seeds) reach test AUCs of 0.905, 0.921, 0.919 and 0.914: the signal is strong, but the differences between models are of the same order as on the precipitation panels. The contrasts are Random Forest against logistic regression and XGBoost and the perceptron against Random Forest, with a margin of 0.01 AUC and CR2 over cells as the primary procedure (\Cref{S-app:external}).

Spatial dependence dominates the uncertainty. On the whole test set the CR2 standard error is 4.4--6.6 times the DeLong one, so that Random Forest against logistic regression ($+0.017$) is not resolved with 10{,}282 rows (CR2 $[-0.016,+0.049]$, $\nu=13.5$) although DeLong gives $[+0.012,+0.021]$. In 1{,}000 small test sets of 19 cells (median 570 rows, like the first panel), drawn from the 39 test cells with 10--60 rows, DeLong covers the reference difference in only 55--63\,\% of draws and resolves the three contrasts in 61, 87 and 48\,\%, including the perceptron against Random Forest, whose reference difference lies within the margin. CR2 covers in 94--99.8\,\% and resolves in 0.2--10.5\,\%. Part of that over-coverage is a design artefact: each small test samples half of the 39 cells without replacement (finite-population factor 0.72). A post hoc version without it, with all 182 test cells as the reference and 19 of them per small test (factor 0.95), gives the same picture: DeLong covers in 41--45\,\%, CR2 in 96--98\,\%. Two of the five recorded predictions held (no excess of CR2 resolutions within the margin, and DeLong resolving at least as often as CR2; the MDE scaling, at the edge of its range and for the same finite-population reason); three failed: the reference population of 39 cells was too small to resolve Random Forest against logistic regression, the power of CR2 for that contrast was 4\,\%, not 80\,\%, and its coverage was above 0.97. The benchmark therefore does not show that the protocol detects real differences with small tests. It shows that, with clustered test units, an iid procedure makes confident decisions whose intervals miss the reference about half of the time, where a calibrated one keeps its coverage, and that the number of independent clusters, not of rows, sets the resolution.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What a Benchmark of This Size Can and Cannot Conclude}

With 529 correlated test months the nominal (DeLong) 80\,\%-power MDE for 15-seed means is 0.022--0.032 AUC for the comparisons between complete classical and hybrid models; the contrasts at the circuit interface have MDEs of 0.011--0.016, and SEAS5 against the climatology 0.057. The observed gaps between the learned models (at most 0.024) are mostly below the first range. This does not mean that the models are equal. Three observations follow. First, on the scale of one AUC, test-sampling variability exceeds seed variability for every model, but for the hybrids the seed share rises to 14--25\,\%, so seed and sampling error must both be reported. Second, near the MDE the verdict depends on the estimand and on the procedure: single-seed verdicts depend on which seeds are compared, and for the seed means DeLong and the primary cluster procedure disagree on Random Forest against the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN}. The disagreement is consistent with a cross-station co-movement within years that the iid estimator ignores, concentrated in the five years with four stations and absent from the second panel. The choice of procedure is a substantive assumption about the dependence between test cases, so it has to be made before the analysis and calibrated on the design, as here; agreement between procedures is a useful diagnostic, but it does not replace that calibration. Third, the references are strong: no learned model improves on the month$\times$station climatology in AUC or Brier score, and with a label that removes the base rates none discriminates.

For the quantum results, three findings fit together. First, the hybrids lie 0.020--0.024 AUC below Random Forest and are not distinguished from circuit-free classical controls at the same interface; training the circuit helps relative to freezing it, so what helps is a trained periodic map of the four angles. Second, that map is classical in practice: each trained circuit is reproduced exactly by 6{,}561 Fourier coefficients per readout, and on the same frozen angles classical heads learn a map that generalizes at least as well as a circuit trained from scratch. Third, the controls are not blind: when the signal is planted by a circuit, the tensor surrogates recover it every time, while a trained circuit beats the linear map in six of ten configurations and the separable map in none. For a 4-qubit circuit all three are expected from the classical-surrogate results \citep{schreiber2023,sweke2025}. The point of the controls is that the benchmark shows it directly, and that the same controls would be needed, and harder to replace by an argument, for circuits whose spectrum or entanglement makes classical surrogates expensive. The evidence does not establish a quantum performance advantage in this case study; it neither shows nor rules out one on larger or richer data.

\subsection{What These Results Do Not Show}
\label{sec:not_show}

Because Random Forest has the highest mean AUC among the learned models, the results could be read as one more instance of tree ensembles outperforming other models on tabular data \citep{grinsztajn2022}. They do not support that reading. Random Forest does not reach the month$\times$station climatology in AUC, it is worse on Brier score as trained and not better after recalibration, and within a station it discriminates no better than the climatology (\Cref{tab:within}). Its advantage over the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} is resolved by the primary procedure before correction, not by DeLong, and not after the Holm correction; it rests on a dependence pattern outside the calibrated mechanisms, and on the second panel it vanishes. What the data support is that none of the learned models, classical or quantum, adds resolvable skill beyond the seasonal and geographic base rate of this panel, and that the differences between them are below what 529 test months can resolve.

Nor do the results show that variational circuits are ineffective in general: the study concerns 4-qubit circuits behind a 4-dimensional classical bottleneck, simulated without noise, on a small panel whose predictable signal is largely seasonal. And they do not show that DeLong or the cluster procedures are wrong in general: each is calibrated under some of the simulated mechanisms and not under others.

\subsection{A Low-Signal Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}

The strongest empirical result of the case study is negative for every statistical model: none improves on the climatology, none discriminates once the base rates are removed, and none adds information to the dynamical forecast. At a one-month lead, with monthly aggregates from four stations and these predictors, the signal available beyond the seasonal and geographic base rate is too small to be detected with this test set; even SEAS5, started on the first day of the target month, is not resolvably better than the climatology on the pooled label. The tuning of the tree ensembles points the same way: the search selected strongly regularized forests (at least 10 rows per leaf), and early stopping on the validation AUC selected very few boosting rounds. Several factors may contribute: the limited predictability of monthly precipitation one month ahead from local antecedent data, and monthly sums that do not resolve the convective episodes that produce much of the heavy precipitation in this region.

This makes the panel a poor testbed for deciding which architecture is better: on these data the answer is none of them. For the question of this paper it is a relevant case rather than a weakness. Small QML benchmarks, including those cited in the Introduction, often compare models whose differences are of this size, and the noise budget, the disagreement between procedures and the resolution rule describe what such a benchmark can conclude whatever the reason for the small differences. The statistical argument does not depend on the panel having little signal; the conclusions about particular architectures do. The external benchmark of \Cref{sec:external} supplies the missing case: with strong signal, the differences between learned models were again a few hundredths, and the clustering of the test units, not the signal, set what small test sets could resolve.

\subsection{Implications for Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:design_implications}

Sample sizes should be planned for a smallest difference of interest fixed in advance, not for the observed gaps. In practice, the MDE of a planned comparison can be computed from the test size, the prevalence and an assumed AUC before any model is trained (\Cref{tab:hm_mde}) and checked afterwards on the archived predictions (\Cref{fig:resolution}). If the differences of interest are below it, more seeds do not help: averaging seeds reduces the seed component but leaves the sampling error unchanged, and a larger test set is the only remedy.

\textbf{The validation partition has the same limit.} Hyperparameter search, early stopping, Platt maps and the SEAS5 blends are all fitted on the same 516 validation rows (36.6\,\% positive). On that partition one AUC has a Hanley--McNeil standard error of about 0.025, and two configurations can be told apart only when they differ by about 0.030--0.048 AUC (correlation 0.9--0.75; \path{exp46_auc_correlation.py}). Model selection therefore operates below the resolution that this paper establishes for the test set: the configurations chosen by the search, and the number of trees chosen by early stopping (2 to 121 across seeds for XGBoost with identical parameters), are not resolvably better than nearby alternatives, which is consistent with tuning having moved the test AUC by amounts that are not resolved. The resolution argument applies to every decision taken on held-out data, not only to the final comparison (\Cref{tab:protocol}, item 6).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Inferential procedures.} The cluster procedures rely on 19 calendar years, of which only the first five contain all four stations, so the number of stations per cluster changes with the period. Their disagreement with DeLong is traced on the first panel to a co-movement of the station contributions within years, which is at the edge of what a permutation null produces and did not recur on the second panel. The primary procedure is calibrated on simulated mechanisms that do not produce this pattern, and its verdicts on these two pairs change with clusters of consecutive years or without single years; a test period with more independent years, or several independent test periods, would be needed to settle them.

\textbf{Hybrid models.} The comparisons concern 4-qubit circuits behind a 4-dimensional classical bottleneck in noiseless simulation; the hybrids have about a quarter of the trainable parameters of the standalone LSTM, so only the comparisons with the interface controls are capacity-matched. The controls were added after the main analysis, and no equivalence margin was specified in advance. The tensor-product surrogates of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} contain the cross-coordinate products only up to degree 2 per coordinate, while the circuit's spectrum extends to degree 4; the planted-signal controls show that on this encoding the low-degree surrogates recover circuit-generated signals, but not that they would for other encodings. The planted signals were recovered at the interface and not by complete models trained on the raw inputs, and the repeated control uses five seeds and four teachers.

\textbf{Data and labels.} Two of the four stations of the first panel have no complete predictors after 2011--2012, so 83\,\% of the test windows come from two stations; filling the pressure records from neighbouring stations or reanalysis was not attempted. Months with more than 3 missing precipitation days have no label, which removes a small, non-random set of months.

\textbf{Baselines and scope.} The tree ensembles were tuned on the validation partition, which XGBoost also uses for early stopping; the search reached a validation AUC of 0.701 (Random Forest) and 0.723 (XGBoost) and moved the test AUC by $+0.001$ and $+0.015$ relative to the untuned configurations, neither difference resolved (\Cref{S-app:hyperparams}). The neural models were not tuned automatically. All models except the climatology were trained with positive-class weighting; the Brier comparisons describe the models as trained and after Platt recalibration. The panel is small, monthly aggregation does not resolve convective episodes, and the chronological split falls in a period of accelerated warming. We evaluate one ansatz family in noiseless simulation; noise during training \citep{wang2021noise} and hardware execution were not studied. The SEAS5 reference uses one dynamical system, 25 members, a $1^\circ$ grid and a bias correction calibrated on 16 hindcast years.

\textbf{Generality.} The empirical conclusions rest on two panels of four Western Mediterranean stations each, which share the split years and the test period. Other climates, targets and test periods remain untested. The sampling argument and the resolution rule transfer to other benchmarks (\Cref{sec:design}), and the external benchmark shows the same inferential problem with strong signal; its reference population was too small to resolve the main contrast, and it was not evaluated prospectively. The findings about particular models and references do not replace validation on further datasets.

\section{Conclusions}
% ============================================================

This paper asked how large a difference a small benchmark with clustered test cases can resolve, and with which inference. On 529 correlated test station-months, the test sample, not the training seed, limits what can be concluded about the classical models: one AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, and the nominal minimum detectable difference of 15-seed means is 0.022--0.032 AUC for the comparisons between complete models (0.011--0.016 at the circuit interface). It falls as $N^{-1/2}$ only if added test cases bring independent information and the covariance between models stays the same; more station-months within the same 19 years need not achieve that. For variational hybrids the seed variance is also substantial, so both sources must be reported.

The inference matters as much as the size. Station-months of the same year are dependent, and the procedure that stays calibrated on this design is CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom. Near the resolution limit DeLong disagrees with it on Random Forest against the LSTM and the hybrids, consistent with a co-movement of stations within years (permutation $p=0.07$ and 0.12), concentrated in the years with four stations, that the iid estimator ignores and that did not recur on a second panel; these verdicts also change with clusters of consecutive years. On an external benchmark with strong signal and spatially clustered test units, the DeLong intervals of small test sets of 19 clusters covered the reference difference in only 41--63\,\% of draws, against 94--99.8\,\% for the calibrated procedure, and the full-test-set CR2 standard error was 4.4--6.6 times the DeLong one.

In the quantum case study, two hybrid models lie 0.020--0.024 AUC below Random Forest and are not distinguished from circuit-free classical controls at the same interface. No advantage of the trained circuit over Fourier surrogates on its angles was detected; the intervals bound it, they do not show equivalence. The trained circuits are reproduced exactly by a classical Fourier expansion, on common frozen angles classical heads generalize at least as well as a newly trained circuit, and with planted circuit signals the tensor surrogates recover the signal in every one of ten configurations, whereas the trained circuit beats only the linear map, and only in six of them. Finite-shot inference costs 0.002 AUC at 1{,}000 shots. No learned model improves on a month$\times$station climatology in AUC or Brier score; with a station- and month-specific threshold none discriminates; and none adds information to the ECMWF SEAS5 forecast, which itself is not resolvably better than the climatology on the pooled label with this test set. On a second panel the predicted resolution and the null results held, and the model ranking did not.

The consequence for QML benchmarking, and for any small benchmark with dependent test cases, is practical: state the estimand, compute the minimum detectable difference before ranking architectures, report seed and sampling error separately, compare against base-rate and operational references and against classical controls at the same interface, and decide whether an observed difference is resolved from its interval under the stated procedure, not from its size relative to the MDE. The MDE is a property of the design, the true difference it would detect with 80\,\% power; an observed difference below it can still exclude zero (Random Forest against \texttt{LSTM+QTL}, $+0.0235$ against an MDE of 0.028), and one above it need not.

\section{Computations}
\label{app:noise_budget}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Estimators.} For two models scored on the same 529 rows, the DeLong estimator gives the covariance of the two AUCs from their structural components \citep{delong1988}; it treats station-months as independent. The block bootstrap resamples the 19 calendar years (2008--2026) with replacement, keeping all stations of a year together, recomputes both AUCs on each resample ($B=2{,}000$) and takes percentile intervals \citep{field2007}; the moving-block variant with blocks of several consecutive years \citep{kunsch1989} is a sensitivity check. The two-way bootstrap draws a calendar-year resample and a resample of the 15 seeds, one draw shared by both models when they share the fitted LSTM of a seed and one per model otherwise, and recomputes the difference of seed-averaged AUCs. The cluster-robust estimator uses the influence values $\psi_i=(\bar v^{A}_{10,i}-\bar v^{B}_{10,i}-\hat\Delta)/n_1$ for positives and $(\bar v^{A}_{01,j}-\bar v^{B}_{01,j}-\hat\Delta)/n_0$ for negatives, where $\bar v$ are the DeLong components averaged over seeds; $\sum_i\psi_i^2$ equals the DeLong variance of $\hat\Delta$ up to factors $(n-1)/n$, and the year sums of $\psi$ give the cluster-robust one. Writing the year sum as $S_u=\sum_s S_{u,s}$ over stations $s$, $\sum_u S_u^2=\sum_{u,s}S_{u,s}^2+\sum_u\sum_{s\ne t}S_{u,s}S_{u,t}$ separates the within-station and cross-station terms; the permutation null permutes the year labels independently within each station (2{,}000 permutations).

\textbf{Seed-mean statistic.} The AUC of one model is the mean of the DeLong structural components over positives and over negatives, so the mean of the 15 AUCs equals the AUC statistic built from components averaged over seeds, and the paired DeLong variance of $\hat\Delta$ follows from the averaged components. This treats the 15 seeds as fixed and so conditions on the fitted models; the two-way bootstrap relaxes this by resampling seeds.

\textbf{Simulation mechanism.} For each replication, years $u=1,\dots,19$ receive a prevalence effect $\tau_\pi\,\eta_u$ on the logit of $\pi=0.41$ and a number of rows of 528 split as equally as possible (or, in the unequal scenario, a multinomial split with Dirichlet$(3)$ shares). Scorers $k\in\{A,B\}$ are $s_{k}=\mu_k y+e_k+\tau_s\,w_u+\tau_m\,v_{k,u}$, with $e_k=\sqrt\rho\,z+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\varepsilon_k$ (so that the scorer noise has correlation $\rho$), $w_u$ a year shift shared by both scorers and $v_{k,u}$ a year shift specific to each scorer; all $\eta_u,w_u,v_{k,u},z,\varepsilon_k$ are standard normal and $\mu_k=\sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{AUC}_k)$ without year effects. The design AUCs without year effects are 0.65 for scorer B and $0.65+\Delta$ for scorer A; the scorer noise has correlation $\rho=0.9$, and the scorer-specific year shifts $v_{k,u}$ are independent between scorers. Scenario B has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=0.3$ and $\tau_m=0$; C adds $\tau_m=0.3$; D is C with Dirichlet year sizes; E has $\tau_\pi=\tau_s=\tau_m=0.6$. The simulation target is the pooled population difference under the mechanism, with years drawn independently and weighted equally, that is, the difference for new years; in scenario D the shares of the 528 rows among years follow a Dirichlet(3) draw that is independent of the year effects, the sample statistic pools observations (so it weights years by their sizes), and the reference is the pooled population AUC with equally likely years of equal size. Scenario D changes the distribution of cluster sizes. Because the sizes are independent of the year effects, the pooled statistic is expected to be centered on the equal-weight reference; this was checked empirically and not proved: in D the mean of the estimates differs from the reference by $+0.0002$, $-0.0008$ and $-0.0004$ for design differences of 0, 0.015 and 0.03 (Monte Carlo standard error of the mean about 0.0008). The population AUC of each scorer is computed as the mean of four independent draws of 400{,}000 rows from the same mechanism with years drawn independently and equally weighted, and the Monte Carlo standard error of the population difference is reported. Because the year shifts are independent of the prevalence effect, the population AUC also has a closed form: in an infinite population of independent years a positive and a negative come from different years with probability 1, so their score difference is normal with mean $\mu_k$ and variance $2(1+\tau_s^2+\tau_m^2)$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC}_k=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_k}{\sqrt{2\,(1+\tau_s^2+\tau_m^2)}}\right).
\end{equation}
This is the AUC for new independent years, not the expected AUC of a fixed set of 19 years, within which pairs from the same year share their shift. At a design difference of 0.03 it gives population differences of 0.0290 (B), 0.0280 (C and D) and 0.0238 (E), in agreement with the Monte Carlo references (\path{exp49_review_minor.py}). When the design difference is 0 the scorers are exchangeable and the population difference is set to 0. DeLong tests use the paired estimator of the seed-free case; the block bootstrap resamples years ($B=300$ per replication).
