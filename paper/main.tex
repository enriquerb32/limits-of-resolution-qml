\begin{abstract}
Small machine-learning benchmarks, including most applied quantum machine learning (QML) studies, rank models by AUC differences of a few hundredths. We ask how large a difference such a benchmark can resolve, and with which inference. The case study forecasts wet months one month ahead at four Mediterranean stations (529 test station-months) with classical models and two hybrids where a classical network feeds a noiseless 4-qubit circuit. One AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, above the seed variability of classical models. Station-months of a year are dependent, so the primary inference is a cluster-robust procedure over the 19 test years, calibrated on the design; under it the minimum detectable difference between 15-seed means is 0.018--0.025 AUC (0.022--0.032 AUC under independence). DeLong, which assumes independence, disagrees near the limit; on an external benchmark with spatial clusters its intervals covered a fixed reference difference in about half of small test sets. Independent information, not row count, sets the resolution. No learned model was resolvably better than a month-by-station climatology or added detectable information to ECMWF SEAS5, and no circuit advantage over classical controls at its interface was detected, though equivalence is not established. A reporting protocol follows, released as a tool.

\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Benchmarking, Statistical Power, Minimum Detectable Effect, Cluster-Robust Inference, Seed Variance, Quantum Machine Learning, Precipitation Forecasting.
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Many comparisons in machine learning are decided by a difference of a few hundredths of AUC on a few hundred test cases. Whether such a difference means anything is mostly a property of the evaluation, not of the models: it depends on how many test cases there are, on how dependent they are, and on the inferential procedure used to compare them. \textbf{The objective of this paper is to make that dependence explicit and usable}: to measure what a small benchmark with clustered test cases can resolve, to choose and validate the inference for it on the design itself, and to turn the result into a reporting protocol that needs only archived predictions. Quantum machine learning (QML) is the case study, because small benchmarks and single-run comparisons are common there and because a claimed quantum contribution can be checked against classical controls.

\textbf{Why it matters.} A single training run confounds initialization luck with architectural merit \citep{henderson2018,bouthillier2021}, and a test on one fixed test set ignores the variance of the training procedure \citep{dietterich1998,nadeau2003}; QML benchmarks add capacity-asymmetric baselines \citep{bowles2024} and variational circuits that are sensitive to initialization even at a few qubits \citep{preskill2018,mcclean2018}. Throughout, ``noise'' means statistical variability, not hardware noise. The standard error of one AUC is set by the size and prevalence of the test set (about 0.024 for the 529 test cases studied here); it is several times the variation between training seeds of the classical models and falls only as $N^{-1/2}$, so more seeds do not remove it. For differences of a few hundredths, the paired standard error, the dependence between test cases and the choice of procedure decide the verdict, and variational hybrids add a seed variance large enough that both sources must be reported.

\textbf{The case study.} A multi-decadal panel of four Western Mediterranean stations, where monthly precipitation is highly variable because much of it falls in a few episodes \citep{romero1999,nieto2005}, is used to forecast one month ahead whether a month will be wet. The panel has what makes small benchmarks hard to read: a few hundred correlated test cases, strong base rates across sites and seasons, and incomplete records. The quantum models are deliberately small: a classical network compresses the inputs to four angles that feed a 4-qubit variational circuit, simulated without noise. It is a testbed for evaluation methodology, not an operational early-warning system.

\textbf{Related work.} Applied weather and climate QML studies \citep{schwabe2025op,schwabe2025,pastori2026,dasilva2026,bazgir2024,fellner2026,kumar2026,marchisio2026} often report quantum models as better than, or as potentially outperforming, classical baselines on the basis of point estimates. \citet{marchisio2026} report a 15.4\,\% lower MSE than a parameter-matched classical neuron on a 2{,}000-sample test set with one value per model; \citet{dasilva2026} show the spread of 10 runs per configuration but compare models through single error values, on test sets of 14 and 5 days. We do not claim that these effects are spurious, only that the evidence reported cannot separate them from sampling and seed noise. Underpowered comparisons and ignored clustering have been documented in NLP, neuroimaging and language-model evaluation \citep{card2020,varoquaux2018,miller2024}. On the quantum side, a circuit that encodes its inputs as angles computes a truncated Fourier series of them \citep{schuld2021fourier}; such models admit classical surrogates \citep{schreiber2023,landman2023,sweke2025}, and trainable variational models are often classically simulable \citep{cerezo2025,thanasilp2024,gilfuster2025}. A classical model with the spectrum of the circuit is therefore the natural control for a claimed quantum contribution, and applied benchmarks rarely include it.

\textbf{Scope.} The paper does not rank architectures. In particular, it does not show that tree ensembles beat quantum or neural models on tabular data: no learned model is resolvably better than a month$\times$station climatology (\Cref{sec:not_show}), and the panel is used for the statistical question, for which small differences between models are the case of interest (\Cref{sec:testbed}). A difference is called \emph{resolved} when the interval of the primary procedure excludes zero (\Cref{sec:multiplicity}); not resolved means that this test set and procedure cannot separate the two models, not that they are equal. The two differences resolved in the primary families, Random Forest against the LSTM and against \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, do not survive the Holm correction or clusters of two consecutive years, do not recur on a second panel and change when the models are refitted with causal imputation, so they are treated as exploratory (\Cref{sec:budget}).

\textbf{Contributions.} The statistical tools are standard; what is new is that each is checked on the design of the study instead of assumed, and that the checks form a protocol.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A noise budget with an explicit estimand}: the difference of seed-averaged AUCs for new years at the same stations, with the historical mixture of eligible station-months; sampling and seed error are reported separately (\Cref{sec:framework,sec:budget}).
    \item \textbf{Inference chosen by calibration.} In a simulation of the real test design, DeLong and the percentile block bootstrap undercover, while CR1, CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom, the jackknife and the wild cluster bootstrap are calibrated within Monte Carlo error when years are independent (none is when serial correlation combines with year$\times$station effects); CR2, analytic and among the best calibrated with ten years, is the primary procedure. Its verdicts are tested against serial dependence, clusters of consecutive years, single influential years and the station mix (\Cref{sec:sampling,sec:budget,sec:simulation}).
    \item \textbf{A resolution rule for benchmark design}: the minimum detectable difference falls as $N^{-1/2}$ when added cases are independent and the covariance between models is unchanged (0.025 AUC at $N=529$ under the primary procedure, 0.032 under independence); a simulation that varies the number of clusters and the rows per cluster separately shows that, with dependence within a cluster, added rows buy diminishing resolution, and beyond a number of rows per cluster set by the dependence only added clusters reduce the MDE. A band recorded before the analysis of a second panel was scaled from an earlier version of the pipeline, and the per-contrast predictions that the panel matches were computed afterwards; an external benchmark with real signal, designed before any fit, shows which decisions small clustered test sets lead to (\Cref{sec:design,sec:replication,sec:external}).
    \item \textbf{Tested quantum controls}: classical and frozen-circuit controls at the circuit's interface, an exact classical emulation of the trained circuits, heads trained on common frozen angles, repeated planted circuit signals with a null, and climatological and operational (ECMWF SEAS5) references (\Cref{sec:applying,sec:reference}).
    \item \textbf{A reporting protocol and a tool} that implements its inferential items from archived predictions, labels, clusters and seeds (\Cref{tab:protocol}).
\end{enumerate}
Together they turn a small benchmark with dependent test cases into a calibrated comparison: state the estimand, select the inference by calibration on the design, separate seed from sampling uncertainty, report what the design can resolve, and attribute a difference to an architectural component, such as a quantum circuit, only through classical controls at its interface whose ability to detect a planted effect has been checked (\Cref{fig:inference_map}).

\section{Data, Task and Models}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Dataset, Target and Split}
\label{sec:dataset_merge}

\textbf{Data.} Daily records of four AEMET stations on the Spanish Mediterranean coast (\Cref{tab:station_coverage}), a transition zone between Mediterranean and semi-arid climates with severe convective episodes \citep{romero1999,nieto2005}, were obtained from \textit{datosclima.es} under the AEMET Open Data framework (downloaded on 1 June 2026) and aggregated to calendar months per station.

\textbf{Task.} Each window uses the 12 preceding months to predict whether the precipitation of the next month exceeds $P_{60}=\qty{29.9}{\milli\meter}$, the pooled 60th percentile of the 2{,}847 labelled station-months up to 1996; the precipitation records, and so the labels used for the threshold, start before the first training window (in 1924 at Barcelona and in 1937--1944 at the other stations). It has 35 features: 12 precipitation lags, 17 physical features built from pressure, temperature, wind, sunshine and FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration \citep{allen1998}, 2 cyclic month terms and 4 station indicators (\Cref{S-app:pipeline}). The label comes from the precipitation series alone and is defined when at most 3 days of the month are missing; with missing days the observed total would be a lower bound of the true one, but every label used here has a complete record (\Cref{S-sec:lookahead}).

\textbf{Split.} The split is chronological (\Cref{fig:design}): training 1938--1996 (2{,}332 windows), validation 1997--2007 (516) and test 2008--2026 (529; 40.6\,\% positive). After 2012 and 2011 the pressure records of Valencia and San Javier no longer give a complete set of predictors, so 83.4\,\% of the test windows come from Alicante and Barcelona. Test base rates differ between stations (27--55\,\%), so part of a pooled AUC reflects telling stations apart (\Cref{sec:reference}). All scaling and anomaly statistics use pre-1997 data only, and the test period falls in a time of accelerated warming.

\textbf{Lookahead.} Three imputation steps of the pipeline used later or full-record values (\Cref{S-sec:lookahead}). A truncation test shows that no test window is affected: rebuilt from the records available at the end of month $t-1$, all 529 test windows have the same inputs and the same eligibility. The lookahead reaches 24 training and 2 validation windows, and retraining the five models of the primary families (the tree ensembles, the LSTM, \texttt{LSTM+QNN} and the Linear-4D control) on a causal panel changes their test AUC by at most 0.002. That is small against the sampling error but not against the resolution limit: refitted causally, Random Forest against the LSTM is no longer resolved (CR2 $p=0.070$ on the 529 common rows) and Random Forest against \texttt{LSTM+QNN} passes the Holm correction ($p=0.009$), while every unresolved comparison stays unresolved (\Cref{S-tab:causal}). The results below use the archived pipeline, which every model, control and reference of the paper shares and which was the primary analysis before the causal refits were made; adopting the causal one after seeing that it moves two verdicts would make the choice of pipeline depend on the results. The causal pipeline is part of the code (\texttt{V2\_CAUSAL=1}). An earlier version of this analysis labelled 23\,\% of the test months from incomplete records; \Cref{S-app:v1} lists the corrections.

\subsection{Models and Fitting Sets}
\label{sec:architectures}

\textbf{Models.} Two references, a month$\times$station climatology fitted on the training partition and the ECMWF SEAS5 seasonal forecast (\Cref{sec:seas5}), are compared with three classical models, Random Forest \citep{breiman2001}, XGBoost and a standalone bidirectional LSTM (10{,}103 parameters), and with two hybrid quantum--classical models simulated without noise in PennyLane (exact expectation values). The tree ensembles were tuned on the validation AUC with declared search spaces (50 trials each); early stopping keeps only 2 to 121 trees in XGBoost across the 15 seeds, so it is a small, heavily regularized ensemble (\Cref{S-app:hyperparams}).

\Cref{fig:architecture} shows the hybrid \texttt{LSTM+QNN}. An LSTM summarizes the 12 precipitation lags; its state and the 35 features are projected to four angles $q\in[0,\pi]^4$, which a 4-qubit circuit with two re-uploading layers maps to four readouts $\langle Z_j\rangle$, followed by LayerNorm and a linear output. Because each angle enters four rotation gates, the circuit computes a truncated Fourier series of the angles with frequencies up to 4 per coordinate, the cross-coordinate products being generated by the entangling gates \citep{schuld2021fourier}. The model has 2{,}613 parameters, 24 of them quantum. \texttt{LSTM+QTL} puts a quantum head on the frozen pre-trained LSTM of the same seed \citep{mari2020}. Circuit details, and a penalized variant, are in \Cref{S-app:architectures,S-app:hybrids}.

\textbf{Controls at the interface.} The controls keep everything of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} except the map applied to the four angles, so that a difference between a control and the hybrid can only come from that map (\Cref{fig:architecture}). The Linear-4D control has a linear $4\to4$ map (2{,}609 parameters against 2{,}613). Fourier surrogates apply a linear map to periodic functions of the angles, either separable ($[\sin kq,\cos kq]_{k\le d}$; \texttt{Fourier-1} to \texttt{Fourier-4}) or with all cross-coordinate products ($\prod_{j}\{1,\cos kq_j,\sin kq_j\}_{k\le d}$; \texttt{Full Fourier-1} and \texttt{-2}, with 81 and 625 features). Two controls keep the circuit with frozen random or zero weights. For \texttt{LSTM+QTL}, \texttt{QTL-classical} and \texttt{QTL tensor-81} replace its circuit by a linear map and by the 81 functions that span exactly what that circuit can compute. Comparisons of a hybrid with Random Forest or with the standalone LSTM are comparisons of whole pipelines and say nothing about the circuit.

\section{Statistical Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Estimand}
\label{sec:estimand}

For models $A$ and $B$ with test probabilities $p_{A,s}$, $p_{B,s}$ from seeds $s=1,\dots,S$ ($S=15$), the statistic is the difference between seed-averaged test AUCs,
\begin{equation}
\hat\Delta=\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{A,s})-\frac1S\sum_{s}\widehat{\mathrm{AUC}}(p_{B,s}),
\end{equation}
and, for single-run comparisons, the difference of two individual AUCs. Both models are scored on the same cases, so they are compared paired \citep{diebold1995,delsole2014}. The estimand is the difference of seed-averaged AUCs for new years at these stations, with the historical mixture of eligible station-months (the station and month composition of the test years, including the change after 2011--2012), conditional on the training data and the fitted models; the observed panel itself is described, not inferred. Variation due to the choice of training set, which can dominate comparisons of learning algorithms \citep{nadeau2003,bouthillier2021,bates2024}, is outside the estimand: the chronological split is fixed, as it is in operational use. A fixed station mix would be a different estimand; restricting the key contrasts to Alicante and Barcelona, the two stations present in every test year, changes no verdict (\Cref{sec:budget}).

\subsection{Sampling Uncertainty}
\label{sec:sampling}

\Cref{fig:inference_map} summarizes the choices. The influence values of $\hat\Delta$ (the seed-averaged DeLong components, centered and divided by the number of positives or negatives) are summed within each calendar year $u$, giving $S_u$. The paired DeLong estimator \citep{delong1988,hanley1982} ignores that grouping and is reported as the reference under independence. The \textbf{primary procedure} is the Bell--McCaffrey CR2 estimator \citep{bell2002}, $\text{SE}_{\text{CR2}}=\{\sum_u S_u^2/(1-n_u/N)\}^{1/2}$, with a $t$ reference whose Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom are $\nu=15.9$ for the unequal year sizes of this test set\footnote{Code and results: \path{qml_resolution.cluster_robust_delta}.}. It was chosen by calibrating every candidate on a simulation of the test design (\Cref{sec:simulation}). With independent years, CR2 covers at 0.935--0.975 in every scenario, and CR1 \citep{obuchowski1997}, the delete-one-year jackknife and the wild cluster bootstrap \citep{webb2023} are calibrated as well within Monte Carlo error; DeLong falls to 0.85 when there are year$\times$station effects, and the percentile year-block bootstrap \citep{field2007} rejects a null difference in 6--13\,\% of replications. Among the calibrated procedures CR2 is chosen because it is analytic and deterministic and among the best calibrated with ten years. With only 19 clusters any cluster variance estimate is noisy \citep{cameron2008}.

No procedure built on calendar-year clusters is calibrated when serial correlation between years combines with year$\times$station effects (coverage 0.87--0.91 for every one). The panel gives no evidence of that regime: for the seven pairs whose verdicts are discussed, the lag-1 autocorrelations of the yearly sums $S_u$ lie between $-0.49$ and $+0.25$ (white-noise band $\pm0.45$; the one value outside it is negative), and Ljung--Box tests over lags 1--3 give $p>0.10$. With 19 years only strong serial correlation would be detected, so clusters of two or three consecutive years \citep{kunsch1989} are a sensitivity check (\Cref{S-app:fewclusters}). Seed uncertainty, on which CR2 conditions, comes from a years$\times$seeds bootstrap; seeds are drawn jointly only when two models share the fitted network of a seed (the heads of \texttt{LSTM+QTL} and the LSTM they are built on), since a common seed number in two separately trained models does not pair them. The ratio of the CR1 to the DeLong standard error (``cluster/iid'') summarizes the within-year dependence, and splitting $S_u$ by station separates within- and cross-station terms (\Cref{sec:budget}).

\subsection{Seed Variability, Minimum Detectable Difference and Decisions}
\label{sec:multiplicity}

For a model with seed standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{seed}}$ and sampling standard error $\text{SE}$ of one AUC, the seed share $\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2/(\sigma_{\text{seed}}^2+\text{SE}^2)$ is a descriptive ratio of two conditional variances. The minimum detectable difference (MDE) at two-sided $\alpha=0.05$ and power 0.80 describes the sensitivity of the design to a true difference; it is not a threshold that an observed difference must exceed, and an observed gap below it can be significant. The MDE reported first is that of the procedure that takes the decision, $(t_{0.975,\nu}+t_{0.80,\nu})\,\text{SE}_{\text{CR2}}$, with the CR2 standard error treated as known, so it inherits the noise of a 19-cluster variance estimate; the DeLong MDE, $2.80\,\text{SE}_{\text{DeLong}}$, is the reference under independence.

Holm correction is applied to the CR2 $p$-values within two families of three seed-mean comparisons (the classical models, and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} against Random Forest, the Linear-4D control and the LSTM); a difference that is to be resolved after correction needs the MDE at the first Holm level, $(t_{1-0.05/6,\nu}+t_{0.80,\nu})\,\text{SE}_{\text{CR2}}$. The remaining analyses, about forty paired comparisons, are secondary and unadjusted. A difference is called \emph{resolved} when its CR2 interval excludes zero and, in the two primary families, \emph{resolved after Holm} when its CR2 $p$-value passes the Holm step; agreement of DeLong with the primary procedure is a diagnostic, not part of the decision. None of these analyses was pre-registered.

\section{Results}
% ============================================================

The results follow the question of the paper in three steps. \Cref{sec:budget,sec:simulation,sec:design} measure what the first panel can resolve: how large the sampling and seed errors are, which procedure is calibrated for this design, and how the resolution scales with the test size. \Cref{sec:applying,sec:reference,sec:stationmonth,sec:seas5} apply that resolution to the quantum case study and to its references: the hybrids against classical pipelines and interface controls, and every learned model against the climatology and the ECMWF forecast. \Cref{sec:replication,sec:external} test the conclusions out of sample, on a second panel and on an external benchmark with real signal.

\subsection{The Noise Budget}
\label{sec:budget}

Seed variability is small for the tree ensembles and the LSTM, but not for the hybrids, where it accounts for 14--25\,\% of the variance. A benchmark of variational models therefore has to report both sources, and adding seeds removes only the smaller one.

\textbf{Why the procedures disagree.} DeLong treats every test month as independent; the cluster procedures treat the months of one calendar year as a block. They differ here only for Random Forest against the neural and hybrid models, whose cluster standard error is 59--78\,\% of the DeLong one (81--142\,\% for pairs of neural or hybrid models; column ``Cluster/iid''). A smaller cluster error means that the advantage of one model is steadier from year to year than independent months would suggest. Splitting each year's contribution by station shows why (\Cref{S-app:cancellation}): in the years in which Random Forest gains on the LSTM at one station, it tends to lose at another, so the station contributions partly cancel within each year. Shuffling the years independently within each station, which breaks that link, brings the ratio back towards 0.75--0.87, and the observed values lie in the lower tail of that distribution ($p=0.07$ and 0.12). The cancellation comes from seasonal patterns that differ between stations and between the two model families, it is concentrated in 2008--2012, the only five years with more than two stations (all four in 2008--2011), and it did not appear on the second panel (\Cref{sec:replication}).

\textbf{How secure the verdicts are.} The cluster procedures are not wrong on this panel, but their extra power over DeLong rests on a pattern that the calibration of the primary procedure does not contain: in the cells with 19 years no simulated mechanism produces cluster/iid ratios as low as 0.59 in more than 1.2\,\% of replications, or as low as 0.72 in more than 5.8\,\%, and we could not construct one that does. The verdicts on Random Forest against the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} are therefore the least secure of the paper. They depend on treating calendar years as independent: with clusters of two consecutive years they are no longer resolved ($p=0.061$ and 0.051), and leaving out a single test year reverses the verdict on Random Forest against the LSTM for 9 of the 19 years. Their nominal verdicts do not depend on the changing station mix: on Alicante and Barcelona alone, present in every test year (441 rows), Random Forest is 0.019 above the LSTM and 0.027 above \texttt{LSTM+QNN} (CR2 $p=0.026$ and 0.014), and no nominal verdict of the pairs discussed here changes; the second $p$-value would also pass the first Holm step, which it does not on the full panel, an exploratory sensitivity rather than a confirmation. They also depend on the fitting pipeline: refitted with causal imputation, Random Forest against the LSTM is no longer resolved ($p=0.070$) and Random Forest against \texttt{LSTM+QNN} passes the Holm correction ($p=0.009$), though not with clusters of three consecutive years ($p=0.076$; \Cref{S-tab:causal}). Other few-cluster procedures (CR1, the jackknife and the wild cluster bootstrap) give the same verdicts, and the effect of freezing the circuit holds for blocks of one to three years and for every omitted year (\Cref{S-app:fewclusters}; all checks in \Cref{S-fig:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Resolution and Test-Set Size}
\label{sec:design}

The resolution limits of \Cref{sec:budget} follow from the size and composition of the test set, so they can be estimated before a benchmark is run. The Hanley--McNeil approximation \citep{hanley1982} gives the standard error of one AUC from the AUC and the class counts alone (0.024 here, matching DeLong); with the correlation between the AUCs of two models \citep{hanley1983} it gives the MDE of a paired difference. For an AUC of 0.68 and a correlation of 0.75, \Cref{tab:hm_mde} gives about 0.049 for $N=500$ at prevalence 0.41 and about 0.086 at prevalence 0.10. These are values under independence, which dependence between test cases can move in either direction. The assumed correlation is conservative for this panel: the DeLong correlation of two single-run AUCs\footnote{Code and results: \path{exp46_auc_correlation.py}.} is 0.78 for Random Forest and \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, 0.86 for Random Forest and the LSTM and 0.91 for the two tree ensembles, and with these values the approximation gives 0.045 and 0.036 for the first two pairs, against empirical MDEs of 0.046 and 0.035 (\Cref{S-fig:resolution}).

Re-evaluating the archived predictions on random subsets of 5 to 16 of the 19 test years (\Cref{S-fig:resolution,S-tab:resolution}) gives MDEs that fall approximately as $N^{-1/2}$, with single-run values 2--26\,\% below the Hanley--McNeil ones. The curve is a consistency check, not independent evidence for the scaling: the subsets overlap, reuse the same fitted models and use the DeLong standard error. Extrapolating the full-test-set standard errors as $N^{-1/2}$, 80\,\% power for a true Random Forest $-$ \texttt{LSTM+QNN} difference of 0.02 needs about 1{,}370 test rows under DeLong (2.6 times the present test set) and about 720 under the cluster-robust standard error, and a difference of 0.01 between the hybrid and the Linear-4D control about 1{,}430 and 1{,}700 rows. These extrapolations assume that the correlation between models and the dependence between years stay as on the panel.

\textbf{Clusters against rows.} The subsets add rows and years together, so they cannot say which of the two buys resolution. A simulation with known truth separates them (\Cref{fig:clusters_rows}): the mechanisms of \Cref{sec:simulation} on designs with $K=10$ to 76 independent clusters of $m=12$ to 96 rows. With independent test cases the MDE falls as $(Km)^{-1/2}$, and rows and clusters are interchangeable. With dependence within a cluster, added rows buy less: fitting $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\Delta)=a/(Km)+b/K$ across designs, the number of rows per cluster at which the two terms are equal, $a/b$, is about 110 with year$\times$station effects and 290--460 with scorer-specific or serially correlated year effects; beyond it only more clusters lower the MDE. CR2 covers at 0.930--0.967 and rejects a null difference in 3.3--7.0\,\% of replications in all 128 cells, whereas DeLong rejects it in up to 17.6\,\% under year$\times$station effects, so its extra power there is not real. Resolution therefore depends on the independent information in the test set, which grows with the number of clusters and, with diminishing returns, with the rows within them. The panels of this paper, with 28 and 47 rows per year, lie where added rows still help under these mechanisms, so the poorer CR2 resolution of the second panel (\Cref{sec:replication}) is not what its size would predict. It goes with the dependence within its years: for the five comparisons whose CR2 MDE grew and whose first-panel ratio is reported (\Cref{tab:paired_ci,tab:qnn_paired}), the cluster/iid ratio rises from 0.59--1.09 to 0.91--1.35, and for \texttt{Fourier-2} against the hybrid, whose MDE fell, it falls from 1.12 to 0.74.

\textbf{Does training the circuit help, and does it help because it is quantum?} Freezing the circuit at random or zero weights lowers the AUC by 0.012--0.013, a difference resolved by the primary procedure (CR2 $p=0.010$--0.014) with which DeLong agrees (\Cref{tab:controls}); only the years$\times$seeds interval, which includes the larger seed variation of the frozen models, reaches zero. Training the circuit therefore helps, for the fitted seeds. The Fourier surrogates, classical maps that apply sines and cosines of the same four angles, all score as well as the trained circuit or slightly better, and no difference is resolved (separable Fourier-1, -3 and -4 in \Cref{S-tab:controls_more}). The 90\,\% cluster-robust bounds limit the difference between the circuit and each surrogate to 0.013--0.018 AUC (\Cref{S-tab:fewclusters}); they are descriptive, not an equivalence test, because no margin was fixed in advance. What the trained circuit adds can thus be obtained from a classical periodic map of the same four angles, within a resolution of about 0.015 AUC: the empirical counterpart of the classical-surrogate argument \citep{schreiber2023,landman2023}.

\textbf{What the circuit computes, and whether it can be replaced.} The end-to-end controls share the interface with the hybrid but not its learned angles, so three questions are separated on the 15 trained circuits (\Cref{S-app:spectrum,S-app:emulation})\footnote{Code and results: \path{exp52_emulation_substitution.py}.}. \emph{Emulation}: each readout is a trigonometric polynomial with frequencies up to 4 per angle, so its values on a grid of $9^4$ angles determine it exactly; pushed through the same LayerNorm and output layer, the reconstructed polynomials reproduce the logits to $3\times10^{-14}$ and the AUC exactly. On the angles the model actually receives, the readouts are close to linear combinations of the 81 products $\prod_j\{1,\cos q_j,\sin q_j\}$ (mean out-of-sample $R^2=0.984$; 0.974 for circuits with random weights), whereas truncating the exact series to frequency 1 over the whole torus costs 0.058 AUC on average: the circuit is simple on the angles it visits, not everywhere. \emph{Substitution and learning on common angles}: with the LSTM and projection of each seed frozen, every head receives exactly the same angles. A circuit trained from scratch on them (15-seed mean AUC 0.652) is below the separable Fourier-2 map (0.664; CR2 $p=0.002$) and the 81- and 625-function tensor maps (0.668 and 0.667; $p=0.001$ and 0.013), and not distinguished from Linear-4D (0.654); the original end-to-end circuit (0.660) is not distinguished from Fourier-2 and lies 0.008 below the 81-function map (CR2 $p=0.035$; the years$\times$seeds interval reaches zero). The circuit can be emulated exactly, a classical surrogate learned on the same angles generalizes at least as well, and the circuit itself is the harder map to learn.

\textbf{Would the controls detect a circuit contribution?} With no learned model above the climatology, the absence of a detected circuit advantage could hold simply because there is nothing for the circuit to learn. We therefore planted signals generated by a circuit (\Cref{S-app:teacher})\footnote{Code and results: \path{exp50_teacher_grid.py}.}: a ``teacher'' circuit with the architecture of \texttt{LSTM+QNN} and fixed random weights scored the real inputs, and new labels were drawn from its score. A first teacher, chosen among 20 because most of its signal (89\,\%) lies in interactions between angles, favoured the circuit over the linear and separable maps (\Cref{S-tab:teacher_single}), but one selected teacher with one label draw is a stress test, not evidence of power. \Cref{tab:teacher_main} repeats the control with four teachers not selected for anything, scored through the same LayerNorm as the students, at two signal strengths, with a second label draw and two null configurations. With the primary procedure on the paired seed means, the trained circuit is above the Linear-4D map in 6 of the 10 configurations, never above the separable Fourier-2 map (which is above it in 2), and below the 81-function tensor surrogate in all 10; no contrast rejects under the null. The tensor surrogates detect a planted circuit signal in every configuration, but a trained circuit head does not learn a circuit-generated function better than its classical surrogates. When complete models must also learn the angles from the raw inputs, no model recovers the planted signal (AUC 0.59--0.62 for every model; \Cref{S-tab:teacher}), which is why the controls at the interface are the relevant ones.

\textbf{Base rates.}\label{sec:stationmonth} Because the threshold is pooled across stations whose base rates differ, a pooled AUC partly rewards telling stations and seasons apart \citep{hamill2006}. Counting only pairs of months from the same station, every learned model loses 0.020--0.032 AUC and the climatology has the highest value (0.667; \Cref{S-tab:within}). With a label defined by the training 60th percentile of each station and calendar month, no model discriminates (\Cref{S-tab:stationmonth}): the 15-seed mean AUCs lie between 0.505 and 0.514, against 0.479 for the climatology, and no paired difference is distinguished from zero (CR2 $p\ge0.72$ between models and $p\ge0.14$ against the climatology; DeLong $p\ge0.70$ between models). That label removes the base rates from the training labels but not by construction from the test labels, so the same question was also asked with the original label: an AUC over pairs of the same station and calendar month, for which the climatology scores exactly 0.5 because it is constant within a cell. Every model scores 0.449--0.495 (year-block intervals include 0.5 for four of the five models; that of \texttt{LSTM+QNN}, $[0.423,0.497]$, lies just below)\footnote{Code and results: \path{exp49_review_minor.py}.}. The predictors therefore carry no detectable information about monthly exceedance beyond the seasonal cycle and the station, and the pooled AUCs of 0.65--0.68 are consistent with the models reproducing those base rates.

\textbf{An operational reference.}\label{sec:seas5} An agency would compare a statistical model with its dynamical forecast, so ECMWF SEAS5 \citep{johnson2019seas5,c3s_seasonal_monthly} enters as a further model (\Cref{S-app:seas5}): 25 members interpolated to the stations, with the exceedance probability, specified before the analysis, taken as the fraction of members above the model's own quantile calibrated on the 1981--1996 hindcasts. Lead 2 (start on the first day of the previous month) is available before the target month and is the operational comparison; lead 1 starts on the first day of the target month and is released several days later, so it is a reference issued after the statistical models, not a demonstrated upper bound. Member fractions are not fair scores \citep{ferro2014}; probabilistic scores use SEAS5 after Platt recalibration.

\subsection{External Benchmark with Real Signal}
\label{sec:external}

Both panels have little signal, so we asked what the protocol decides when the models do discriminate. The design, the contrasts, a margin of relevance of 0.01 AUC and five predictions were committed before any model was fitted (\path{audit/prediction_external.txt}; \Cref{S-app:external}). The data are the 20{,}640 block groups of California Housing \citep{pace1997}, the target a median house value above the training median, the clusters spatial cells of $0.25^\circ$, and the split is by cell (182 test cells, 10{,}282 rows). Logistic regression, Random Forest, XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron (5 seeds) reach test AUCs of 0.905, 0.921, 0.919 and 0.914: the signal is strong, but the differences between models are of the same order as on the precipitation panels.

Spatial dependence dominates the uncertainty. On the whole test set the CR2 standard error is 4.4--6.6 times the DeLong one, so that Random Forest against logistic regression ($+0.017$) is not resolved with 10{,}282 rows (CR2 $[-0.016,+0.049]$, $\nu=13.5$), although DeLong gives $[+0.012,+0.021]$. In 1{,}000 small test sets of 19 cells (median 570 rows, like the first panel), drawn from the 39 test cells with 10--60 rows, the reference is the difference on those 39 cells, a fixed, finite set, not new spatial cells. DeLong covers the reference difference in only 55--63\,\% of draws and resolves the three contrasts in 61, 87 and 48\,\%, including the perceptron against Random Forest, whose reference difference lies within the margin; in 16\,\% of draws it excludes zero in the direction opposite to that fixed reference, whose own sign is not determined. CR2 covers in 94--99.8\,\% and resolves in 0.2--10.5\,\%. Part of the over-coverage of CR2 comes from sampling half of 39 cells without replacement; with all 182 test cells as the reference (post hoc), DeLong covers in 41--45\,\%, CR2 in 96--98\,\%. Three of the five recorded predictions failed: the reference population of 39 cells was too small to resolve Random Forest against logistic regression, the power of CR2 for that contrast was 4\,\%, not 80\,\%, and its coverage was above 0.97. The benchmark therefore does not show that the protocol detects real differences with small tests. It shows that, with clustered test units, an iid procedure makes many more nominal decisions, with intervals that miss a fixed reference about half of the time, where the cluster procedure keeps its coverage of that reference, and that with spatially clustered test units 10{,}282 rows did not resolve a difference of 0.017.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{What a Benchmark of This Size Can Conclude}

With 529 correlated test months the 80\,\%-power MDE for 15-seed means under the primary procedure is 0.018--0.025 AUC for the comparisons between complete classical and hybrid models (0.022--0.032 under independence), 0.012--0.019 at the circuit interface (0.011--0.016), and 0.057 for SEAS5 against the climatology under independence. The observed gaps between the learned models (at most 0.024) are mostly below the first range, which does not mean that the models are equal. Three lessons follow. First, test-sampling variability exceeds seed variability for every model on the scale of one AUC, but for the hybrids the seed share rises to 14--25\,\%, so both sources must be reported. Second, near the MDE the verdict depends on the estimand and on the procedure: single-seed verdicts depend on which seeds are compared, and for the seed means DeLong and the primary cluster procedure disagree on Random Forest against the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN}. The choice of procedure is a substantive assumption about the dependence between test cases, so it has to be made before the analysis and calibrated on the design; agreement between procedures is a useful diagnostic, not a substitute for that calibration. Third, the references are strong: no learned model is resolvably better than the month$\times$station climatology (none scores above it), and with a label that removes the base rates none discriminates detectably.

For the quantum results, three findings fit together. The hybrids are not distinguished from circuit-free controls at the same interface, and training the circuit helps relative to freezing it, so what helps is a trained periodic map of the four angles. That map is classical in practice: each trained circuit is reproduced exactly by 6{,}561 Fourier coefficients per readout, and on the same frozen angles classical heads generalize at least as well as a circuit trained from scratch. And the controls are not blind: when a circuit plants the signal, the tensor surrogates recover it every time, while a trained circuit beats the linear map in six of ten configurations and the separable map in none. For a 4-qubit circuit all three are expected from the classical-surrogate results \citep{schreiber2023,sweke2025}; the point of the controls is that the benchmark shows it directly, and that the same controls would be needed, and harder to replace by an argument, for circuits whose spectrum or entanglement makes classical surrogates expensive. The general lesson is methodological: a quantum contribution cannot be read from a comparison of a hybrid with a classical pipeline; it has to be tested against classical surrogates matched in interface and capacity, with planted signals showing that the comparison could have detected a real circuit effect. The quantum findings themselves are limited to small, noiseless circuits behind a low-dimensional classical bottleneck, and do not extend to larger or noisy circuits or to high-dimensional encodings without such a bottleneck.

\subsection{What the Results Do Not Show}
\label{sec:not_show}
\label{sec:testbed}

They do not show that tree ensembles beat quantum or neural models on tabular data \citep{grinsztajn2022}. Random Forest does not reach the climatology in AUC, it is worse on the Brier score as trained and not better after recalibration, and within a station it discriminates no better than the climatology (\Cref{S-tab:within}); its advantage over the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} is resolved by the primary procedure only outside its calibrated range, not after the Holm correction, and it vanishes on the second panel. Nor do they show that variational circuits are ineffective in general, since the study concerns 4-qubit circuits behind a 4-dimensional classical bottleneck, simulated without noise; or that DeLong or the cluster procedures are wrong in general, since each is calibrated under some mechanisms and not others. The panel is a poor testbed for choosing an architecture: at a one-month lead, with monthly aggregates and these predictors, the signal beyond the seasonal and geographic base rate is too small to detect, and even SEAS5 is not resolvably better than the climatology. For the question of this paper that is a relevant case rather than a weakness, because small QML benchmarks often compare models whose differences are of this size; and the external benchmark supplies the case with strong signal, where the clustering of the test units, not the signal, set what small test sets could resolve.

\subsection{Implications for Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:design_implications}

Test sizes should be planned for a smallest difference of interest fixed in advance, not for the observed gaps: the MDE of a planned comparison can be computed from the test size, the prevalence and an assumed AUC before any model is trained (\Cref{tab:hm_mde}), with the effective number of independent observations, which for grouped test cases lies between the number of clusters and the number of rows (\Cref{sec:design}), as the effective size. If the differences of interest are below the MDE, more seeds do not help; only a larger test set with more independent clusters does. The validation partition has the same limit: on its 516 validation rows (36.6\,\% positive) one AUC has a Hanley--McNeil standard error of about 0.025, and two configurations can be told apart only when they differ by about 0.030--0.048 AUC\footnote{Code and results: \path{exp46_auc_correlation.py}.}, so hyperparameter search and early stopping operate below the resolution of the test set, and the resolution argument applies to every decision taken on held-out data.

\subsection{Recommended Reporting Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\Cref{tab:protocol} lists the practices that follow from the results. Items 1--4 and 10 need only the test probabilities of the models and are implemented in an open library; item 5 also needs validation predictions to recalibrate; items 6--8 concern how the models and labels are built, item 7 requires fitting the controls, and item 9 is a decision taken before the analysis. As a rule of thumb, DeLong suffices when the test cases are independent given the models; when they are grouped (sites, years, patients, documents), the primary interval should be a small-sample cluster procedure such as CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom, reported with the number of clusters, and with clusters of consecutive periods and the wild cluster bootstrap as sensitivity checks when the groups may be serially dependent.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The cluster procedures rely on 19 calendar years, of which only the first five contain more than two stations and only the first four all four; the verdicts on Random Forest against the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} rest on a co-movement of stations that the calibration does not contain and change with clusters of consecutive years or without single years, so a test period with more independent years would be needed to settle them. The primary analysis uses the archived pipeline, whose imputation looked ahead for 24 training and 2 validation windows; only the five models of the primary families were refitted causally, and the refit moves both exploratory verdicts. The quantum comparisons concern 4-qubit circuits in noiseless simulation; only the comparisons with the interface controls are capacity-matched, the controls were added after the main analysis, no equivalence margin was fixed in advance, and the planted-signal controls use five seeds and four teachers. Two stations have no complete predictors after 2011--2012, so 83\,\% of the test windows come from two stations; months with more than 3 missing precipitation days have no label; the daily series come from \textit{datosclima.es} and were not cross-checked against the AEMET OpenData service, and station inhomogeneities remain \citep{alexandersson1986,peterson1998}. The tree ensembles were tuned on the validation partition (moving the test AUC by $+0.001$ and $+0.015$, neither resolved; \Cref{S-app:hyperparams}), the neural models were not tuned automatically, and all models except the climatology were trained with positive-class weighting. Noise during training \citep{wang2021noise} and hardware execution were not studied, and the SEAS5 reference uses one system with 25 members. The empirical conclusions rest on two panels that share the region and the test years and on one external benchmark, whose reference population was too small to resolve its main contrast and which was not evaluated prospectively.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% ============================================================

This paper asked how large a difference a small benchmark with clustered test cases can resolve, and with which inference. On 529 correlated test station-months the test sample, not the training seed, limits what can be concluded: one AUC has a standard error of about 0.024, and the minimum detectable difference of 15-seed means under the primary procedure is 0.018--0.025 AUC for the comparisons between complete models (0.012--0.019 at the circuit interface; 0.022--0.032 and 0.011--0.016 under independence). It falls as $N^{-1/2}$ only if added test cases bring independent information: resolution depends on how much independent information the test cases carry, not on the row count alone (\Cref{sec:design,sec:replication}).

The inference matters as much as the size. Station-months of the same year are dependent, and CR2 with Bell--McCaffrey degrees of freedom was chosen among the procedures that are calibrated on this design when years are independent; it undercovers when serial correlation combines with year$\times$station effects, and its calibration under the cancellation pattern that separates it from DeLong on this panel is not established, because no simulated mechanism reproduces that pattern. On an external benchmark with strong signal and spatially clustered test units, the DeLong intervals of small test sets of 19 clusters covered a fixed reference difference in only 41--63\,\% of draws, against 94--99.8\,\% for the cluster procedure.

In the quantum case study, the hybrids are not distinguished from classical controls at the same interface, and no advantage of the trained circuit over Fourier surrogates was detected; the intervals bound it, they do not show equivalence. The trained circuits are reproduced exactly by a classical Fourier expansion, and with planted circuit signals the tensor surrogates recover the signal in every one of ten configurations, whereas the trained circuit beats only the linear map, and only in six of them. No learned model is resolvably better than a month$\times$station climatology or adds detectable information to the ECMWF SEAS5 forecast. The two differences resolved in the primary families, Random Forest above the LSTM and \texttt{LSTM+QNN} on the first panel, are exploratory: they do not survive the Holm correction or clusters of consecutive years, do not recur on the second panel, and a causal refit that changes each AUC by at most 0.002 moves both, one below the resolution limit and one past the Holm threshold. Near that limit a verdict is not stable even to the fitting pipeline.

The consequence for QML benchmarking, and for any small benchmark with dependent test cases, is practical: state the estimand, choose and calibrate the inference on the design before the analysis, compute the minimum detectable difference of that procedure before ranking architectures, report seed and sampling error separately, compare against base-rate and operational references and against classical controls at the same interface, and decide whether a difference is resolved from its interval, not from its size relative to the MDE. The MDE is a property of the design: an observed difference below it can still exclude zero (Random Forest against the LSTM, $+0.015$ against a primary MDE of 0.020), and one above it need not.

\textbf{Estimators.} For two models scored on the same 529 rows, the DeLong estimator gives the covariance of the two AUCs from their structural components \citep{delong1988}; it treats station-months as independent. The block bootstrap resamples the 19 calendar years (2008--2026) with replacement, keeping all stations of a year together, recomputes both AUCs on each resample ($B=2{,}000$) and takes percentile intervals \citep{field2007}; the moving-block variant with blocks of several consecutive years \citep{kunsch1989} is a sensitivity check. The two-way bootstrap draws a calendar-year resample and a resample of the 15 seeds, one draw shared by both models when they share the fitted LSTM of a seed and one per model otherwise, and recomputes the difference of seed-averaged AUCs. The cluster-robust estimator uses the influence values $\psi_i=(\bar v^{A}_{10,i}-\bar v^{B}_{10,i}-\hat\Delta)/n_1$ for positives and $(\bar v^{A}_{01,j}-\bar v^{B}_{01,j}-\hat\Delta)/n_0$ for negatives, where $\bar v$ are the DeLong components averaged over seeds; $\sum_i\psi_i^2$ equals the DeLong variance of $\hat\Delta$ up to factors $(n-1)/n$, and the year sums of $\psi$ give the cluster-robust one. Writing the year sum as $S_u=\sum_s S_{u,s}$ over stations $s$, $\sum_u S_u^2=\sum_{u,s}S_{u,s}^2+\sum_u\sum_{s\ne t}S_{u,s}S_{u,t}$ separates the within-station and cross-station terms; the permutation null permutes the year labels independently within each station (2{,}000 permutations).

\textbf{Seed-mean statistic.} The AUC of one model is the mean of the DeLong structural components over positives and over negatives, so the mean of the 15 AUCs equals the AUC statistic built from components averaged over seeds, and the paired DeLong variance of $\hat\Delta$ follows from the averaged components. This treats the 15 seeds as fixed and so conditions on the fitted models; the two-way bootstrap relaxes this by resampling seeds.
